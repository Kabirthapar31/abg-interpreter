Before you start

Read this first.

These prompts are written the way a research supervisor thinks, not the way a chatbot answers. Many are long on purpose: a vague prompt gets you a vague, confident, wrong answer. Before you paste anything, five non-negotiables.

01
The AI does not know your patients.Every prompt has a context block. Fill it honestly. If you say you have 200 cases a year when you have 40, the model will design a study you cannot finish.
02
Never accept a citation you have not opened.Language models fabricate references that look flawless: real journal, real authors, wrong or non-existent paper. Verify every reference on PubMed by PMID or DOI before it enters your work.
03
Never paste identifiable patient data.No names, hospital IDs, MRNs or scans with burned-in identifiers. De-identify before the data touches a chatbot. This is an ethics committee issue, not a preference.
04
The AI drafts. You decide.Use it to speed up structure, phrasing and pre-checking. The scientific judgement, the assumptions and the interpretation stay yours. Your name goes on the paper, not the model's.
05
Declare AI use where required.ICMJE and most journals require disclosure of generative AI use. AI cannot be an author. Check your journal's policy and your university's thesis rules before submission.

How to use each prompt

  • Copy the full prompt. The length is what makes the output specific.
  • Replace every [SQUARE BRACKET] with your real details. Options inside a bracket are separated by "/"; keep the one that applies.
  • Put anything you paste (abstracts, drafts, results) where the prompt shows [PASTE …], above the task.
  • Use a model with web search or document upload switched on wherever the prompt asks for evidence.
  • Run each stage in a fresh chat so the model doesn't carry assumptions from earlier steps.
  • Push back. If the output is generic, reply: "Be more specific. Tie every point to my context."
The framework

Every prompt is built on P-R-O-M-P-T.

One formula, six layers. Each layer matches what OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Microsoft recommend in their own prompting guides.

R

O

M

401Mixed-audience seminar deck, clinically rigorous
Master
2.1 Seminar preparation · PG Resident / Faculty
Use itWhen you have a departmental seminar on any topic and want a complete, evidence-based deck.
WhyDepartmental audiences are mixed: MBBS students, interns, residents and consultants in the same room. A deck pitched at resident level, with a short foundation recap for juniors and a controversies section for consultants, keeps everyone engaged, and cites real evidence rather than textbook generalities.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a senior academic in [SPECIALTY] who has delivered and moderated hundreds of departmental seminars. You are clinically rigorous, evidence-based and practical, and you never invent citations or statistics.
My context
Topic: [e.g. Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: from pathophysiology to ventilator management]
Audience: mixed (MBBS students, interns, junior residents, senior residents, consultants)
Time available: [e.g. 45 minutes + 15 minutes discussion]
Sources I have (guidelines, reviews, textbook chapters), uploaded or listed: [UPLOAD OR LIST]
Output format: [slide-by-slide text for PowerPoint / a .pptx file if your tool can create one]
Task
1. Pitch the core content at resident level, with a 1–2 slide foundation recap early, and advanced nuance for consultants.
2. Follow this structure, adapting as needed: title slide with a one-line clinical hook; why this matters (a representative case); foundation recap (definitions, classification); pathophysiology; clinical features and diagnostic approach (criteria or scores in tables); management, stepwise, first-line versus escalation; an evidence corner (landmark trials and current guidelines); controversies and grey zones; 3–5 key takeaways; references in Vancouver style.
3. For every slide give a message title, concise bullets (one idea per slide), a table where comparisons help, and speaker notes.
4. Mark what is exam-relevant for students and what is decision-changing for residents and consultants.
5. Suggest where a figure, flowchart or algorithm should go, and describe it.
Rules
Name trials and guidelines only from my sources or with [VERIFY] beside each, because misremembered trial names and years are the most embarrassing seminar error. Mark every drug dose as [VERIFY DOSE FROM CURRENT GUIDELINE]. Show me the slide-by-slide text for review before building any file.
Output
A slide table (number, title, bullets, visual, speaker notes, audience tag), then the reference list with [VERIFY] flags.
Tweak
End with the 5 questions a consultant is most likely to ask after this seminar, and short evidence-based answers.

How to get more out of it

  • Tuning levers: change the depth (resident ↔ consultant), the slide count (about 1 slide per minute) and the visual style.
  • Verify every trial and guideline in the evidence corner against the original source before presenting.
  • Rehearse the controversies section. It is where the discussion will go.

402From topic to seminar blueprint in one sitting
Master
2.1 Seminar preparation · PG Resident
Use itThe day your seminar topic is assigned, before you open any textbook.
WhyMost residents start a seminar by reading everything and end with 80 slides and no structure. Starting with objectives, a time budget and a storyline tells you exactly what to read and what to leave out.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a medical educator and senior faculty member who mentors residents on seminar preparation. You think backwards from what the audience must be able to do after the session.
My context
Topic as assigned: [e.g. Approach to hyponatraemia]
Specialty and my year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and its level: [brief]
Duration: [minutes]
Days until the seminar: [N]
Moderator's known expectations or style: [brief or "unknown"]
Task
1. Clarify the scope: what is in, what is out, and how to phrase the final seminar title.
2. Write 4–6 learning objectives using Bloom's verbs (explain, differentiate, apply, choose).
3. Build a storyline: an opening case, 5–7 content blocks mapped to the objectives, and a closing return to the case.
4. Create a time budget per block that fits the duration.
5. For each block, list the must-read sources by type (current guideline, recent review, textbook chapter) with [VERIFY LATEST EDITION].
6. Make a day-by-day preparation plan for the days I have, including a rehearsal.
7. Predict the moderator's 5 likely questions.
Rules
Keep the content proportional to the time, about one slide per minute, and cut anything not tied to an objective, because overlong seminars lose the audience in the middle. Name no specific papers from memory; describe the source type and mark it [FIND AND VERIFY].
Output
Scope note, objectives, storyline table (block, objective, minutes, key points), the source list, the preparation plan, then the predicted questions.
Tweak
End with the single case vignette to open with, 60 words, de-identified and realistic for Indian practice.

How to get more out of it

  • Show the objectives and storyline to your moderator a week before. It avoids a last-minute restructure.
  • Read the guideline first, the review second and the textbook last.
  • Keep the opening case alive: return to it in every block.

403Evidence pack: guidelines and trials, verified from my sources
Master
2.1 Seminar preparation · PG Resident / Faculty
Use itWhen your seminar needs an evidence corner and you have gathered the guidelines and key papers.
WhyThe evidence section is where seminars go wrong: trials cited with the wrong year, outcomes overstated, or guidelines quoted from memory. Building it strictly from uploaded sources, with quotes, makes it accurate and defensible when a consultant challenges it.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are an evidence-based medicine specialist who prepares evidence summaries for clinical guideline panels. You quote the source for every claim and state the certainty of evidence clearly.
My context
Seminar topic and the clinical questions it must answer: [list 3–5 questions]
Guidelines uploaded (name, organisation, year): [UPLOAD / LIST]
Key trials and meta-analyses uploaded: [UPLOAD / LIST]
Setting to apply the evidence to: [e.g. Indian tertiary and district hospitals]
Audience and how much time the evidence section gets: [e.g. residents and consultants, 8 minutes]
My specialty and year: [brief]
Task
1. For each clinical question, summarise what the uploaded guidelines recommend, with the strength or grade, quoting the recommendation wording.
2. Build a trials table: trial, year, design, population, intervention versus comparator, primary outcome and result with CI, and the key limitation.
3. Identify where guidelines disagree with each other, and why.
4. Identify where the evidence does not apply well to Indian settings (population, resources, drug availability).
5. Write 3 evidence-corner slides with speaker notes.
6. List any major trial or guideline the audience will expect that is missing from my uploads, marked [FIND AND UPLOAD], so I can add it before the seminar.
Rules
Use only the uploaded documents; for anything else write [NOT IN MY SOURCES — VERIFY], because a single wrong trial detail can discredit the whole seminar. Reproduce effect sizes exactly. State certainty levels as the guideline states them.
Output
A recommendations table per question, the trials table, the disagreement notes, the applicability notes, then the slide text.
Tweak
End with the one piece of evidence I should read in full before presenting, because it is most likely to be questioned.

How to get more out of it

  • Upload the guideline PDFs themselves, not summaries of them.
  • Put the year on every trial and guideline on the slides. Consultants check it.
  • If two guidelines disagree, show both. That is where the best discussion happens.

404Full speaker script for a 45-minute seminar
Master
2.1 Seminar preparation · PG Resident
Use itWhen your slides are ready and you want to rehearse to time.
WhyReading slides aloud is the commonest seminar failure. A script written for speaking, with timing, transitions and planned interaction, lets you rehearse properly, then speak from notes on the day.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a speaking coach for medical educators who writes natural, spoken scripts and trims them to the second.
My context
My slides (titles and bullets, or upload the file): [PASTE OR UPLOAD]
Duration of the talk (excluding discussion): [e.g. 40 minutes]
Audience: [brief]
My speaking style and my nerves: [brief]
Points where I want audience interaction: [brief or "suggest"]
Parts of the content I find hardest to explain: [list]
Language mix the audience is comfortable with: [e.g. English with occasional Hindi]
Task
1. Write a spoken script per slide at about 130 words per minute, in conversational academic English.
2. Mark timing checkpoints (e.g. "by slide 10: 12 minutes").
3. Add a transition sentence between sections, so the story flows.
4. Insert 3–4 interaction moments: a question to the audience, a quick show of hands, or a case pause.
5. Mark the lines to memorise word for word (opening, key message, closing).
6. Flag slides whose script runs long, and suggest cuts.
7. For the hardest parts I listed, write a simpler explanation or an analogy I can use if the audience looks lost.
8. Write a 30-second emergency version of the conclusion in case time runs out.
Rules
Keep facts exactly as on my slides and add no new data, because the script must match what the audience sees. Script the interaction moments with a fallback answer in case nobody responds.
Output
A script table (slide, script, seconds, interaction), then the checkpoints and the memorise list.
Tweak
End with a 5-step rehearsal plan for the next 3 days.

How to get more out of it

  • Rehearse aloud with a timer. Silent reading always underestimates the time.
  • Don't read the script on the day. Speak from the checkpoints and the memorised lines.
  • Record one rehearsal on your phone. You'll hear your filler words immediately.

405Case-based interactive seminar
Master
2.1 Seminar preparation · PG Resident / Faculty
Use itWhen you want a seminar built around an unfolding case rather than a lecture.
WhyCase-based seminars keep the audience thinking instead of copying. Revealing a case in stages, with questions at each stage, teaches clinical reasoning and makes the content stick.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a clinician-educator who designs case-based learning sessions for residents, and who knows how to reveal information at the right pace.
My context
Topic: [TOPIC]
Learning objectives: [PASTE OR "suggest"]
Audience and number: [brief]
Duration: [minutes]
A real case I can adapt (de-identified), or "create a realistic one": [PASTE OR REQUEST]
Setting where the case takes place: [emergency / ward / OPD / district hospital]
Common mistakes residents make with this condition: [list or "suggest"]
Task
1. Build an unfolding case in 4–6 stages: presentation, examination, initial investigations, key result, management decision, and follow-up or complication.
2. At each stage, write 1–2 questions for the audience, the expected answers, and the teaching point to deliver.
3. Map each stage to a learning objective.
4. Add a short mini-lecture slide (3 bullets) after each stage to consolidate.
5. Close with a summary of the reasoning path, and take-home points.
6. Suggest how to run it: small groups, polls or calling on people.
7. Build at least one stage around a common mistake, so the audience can discover why it is wrong.
8. Give the timing for each stage so the whole case fits the duration.
Rules
Keep the case realistic for Indian practice and fully de-identified. Mark drug doses and guideline specifics as [VERIFY], because case-based sessions invite detailed questions.
Output
A stage table (stage, information revealed, questions, expected answers, teaching point, objective), the mini-lecture slides, then the facilitation guide.
Tweak
End with 2 variations of the case (a different age or comorbidity) for repeat sessions.

How to get more out of it

  • Reveal information on separate slides. Showing everything at once kills the reasoning.
  • Wait 10 seconds after asking a question. Silence is thinking time.
  • Keep a "parking lot" for tangent questions and answer them at the end.

406Mock moderator: grill me before the real seminar
Master
2.1 Seminar preparation · PG Resident
Use it2–3 days before your seminar, once the slides are final.
WhyThe dreaded part of a seminar is the moderator's questions. A realistic mock session, one question at a time with feedback, turns fear into preparation and shows you exactly which slides need strengthening.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a senior professor and seminar moderator known for probing questions on basics, recent evidence and practical application. You are strict but fair, and you give frank feedback.
My context
Seminar topic and my specialty and year: [brief]
My slide titles and key content: [PASTE]
Areas I feel weakest in: [list]
My moderator's known style or favourite themes: [brief or "unknown"]
Duration of the seminar and the discussion slot: [brief]
Task
1. Ask me one question at a time, mixing basic science, clinical application, recent evidence, Indian context and "what would you do" scenarios.
2. After each answer, score it out of 10, give the ideal answer in 3–5 points, and tell me which slide should cover it better.
3. Include 2 classic trap questions moderators use on this topic.
4. Mix difficulty: start with 3 basic questions, then 4 applied questions, then 3 advanced or evidence questions.
5. After 10 questions, summarise my weak areas and the slides to strengthen, and give me a one-day plan to fix them.
Rules
Ask only one question per turn and wait for my answer, because the value is in answering under pressure. Base questions on my topic and slides. Mark any fact in your ideal answers that depends on a guideline as [VERIFY].
Output
Conversational: one question per turn, feedback in a short structured block (score, ideal answer, gap, slide to fix), then the final summary with the weak areas ranked from most to least urgent.
Tweak
End with the 3 questions I should prepare backup slides for.

How to get more out of it

  • Answer aloud before typing. The seminar is spoken.
  • Add backup slides at the end of the deck for the questions you struggled with.
  • Ask a colleague to play the moderator with these questions for a real rehearsal.

407Turn a new guideline into a seminar
Master
2.1 Seminar preparation · PG Resident / Faculty
Use itWhen a new or updated clinical guideline is released and your department wants a session on it.
Why"What's new in the guideline" seminars are common and often done badly: a slide per recommendation, read aloud. The useful version shows what changed, why, what it means for practice here, and what remains uncertain.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a guideline methodologist and clinician who explains guideline updates to busy clinicians, focusing on what changes practice.
My context
The guideline (upload the full text or the recommendations section): [UPLOAD]
The previous version, if available: [UPLOAD OR "not available"]
Audience and duration: [brief]
Local practice for this condition in my hospital: [brief]
Drugs, tests or devices the guideline recommends that my hospital lacks: [list or "unsure"]
My specialty and year: [brief]
Task
1. List the key recommendations with their strength and certainty, quoting the exact wording and the section number.
2. Identify what is new or changed since the previous version, and the evidence cited for each change.
3. Classify each change as practice-changing, practice-confirming or minor.
4. Discuss applicability to Indian settings: resources, drug availability, cost and staffing.
5. Build a 20–30 minute slide plan: what changed, why, what to do on Monday, and uncertainties.
6. Write a one-page summary handout.
7. Suggest a simple audit the department could run to check adherence to the key recommendation.
8. Note which recommendations are conditional or based on low-certainty evidence, so the audience doesn't treat them as absolute.
Rules
Quote only from the uploaded guideline and mark anything else [VERIFY], because a misquoted recommendation can change patient care. Don't overstate the certainty of weak recommendations.
Output
The recommendations table, the changes table (old position, new position, evidence, classification), the applicability notes, the slide plan with timings, then the handout.
Tweak
End with the 3 recommendations most likely to be debated in the department, and why.

How to get more out of it

  • Lead with what changes practice. Nobody remembers the minor edits.
  • Bring the guideline's own algorithm figure if it has one, and credit it.
  • Invite a senior to comment on local applicability. It turns the seminar into a discussion.

Master
2.6 Case presentations & grand rounds · PG Resident / MBBS Intern
Use itWhen you present a case at a departmental case discussion or clinical meeting.
WhyCase presentations are where residents show clinical reasoning. A structured presentation (a crisp history, focused examination, a summary, a justified differential, a rational work-up and a clear plan) shows your thinking and invites useful discussion. A disorganised one invites interruption.

Persona

Teaching with AI, responsibly
  • Verify every drug dose, guideline year and trial detail before it goes on a slide.
  • Never put identifiable patient data or images into AI tools or slides.
  • Credit figures you adapt, and use only images you have the right to use.
  • AI drafts the deck. You own the content, the delivery and the answers.
