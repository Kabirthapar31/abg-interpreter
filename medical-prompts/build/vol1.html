Before you start

Read this first.

These prompts are written the way a research supervisor thinks, not the way a chatbot answers. Many are long on purpose: a vague prompt gets you a vague, confident, wrong answer. Before you paste anything, five non-negotiables.

01
The AI does not know your patients.Every prompt has a context block. Fill it honestly. If you say you have 200 cases a year when you have 40, the model will design a study you cannot finish.
02
Never accept a citation you have not opened.Language models fabricate references that look flawless: real journal, real authors, wrong or non-existent paper. Verify every reference on PubMed by PMID or DOI before it enters your work.
03
Never paste identifiable patient data.No names, hospital IDs, MRNs or scans with burned-in identifiers. De-identify before the data touches a chatbot. This is an ethics committee issue, not a preference.
04
The AI drafts. You decide.Use it to speed up structure, phrasing and pre-checking. The scientific judgement, the assumptions and the interpretation stay yours. Your name goes on the paper, not the model's.
05
Declare AI use where required.ICMJE and most journals require disclosure of generative AI use. AI cannot be an author. Check your journal's policy and your university's thesis rules before submission.

How to use each prompt

  • Copy the full prompt. The length is what makes the output specific.
  • Replace every [SQUARE BRACKET] with your real details. Options inside a bracket are separated by "/"; keep the one that applies.
  • Put anything you paste (abstracts, drafts, results) where the prompt shows [PASTE …], above the task.
  • Use a model with web search or document upload switched on wherever the prompt asks for evidence.
  • Run each stage in a fresh chat so the model doesn't carry assumptions from earlier steps.
  • Push back. If the output is generic, reply: "Be more specific. Tie every point to my context."
The framework

Every prompt is built on P-R-O-M-P-T.

One formula, six layers. Each layer matches what OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Microsoft recommend in their own prompting guides.

P
Persona
Who the AI should be: a senior expert with a trait
R
Request
One clear task, as numbered steps
O
Output
Shape, length, tone, tables
M
Material
Your context and anything you paste
P
Parameters
Rules, guidelines, accuracy guards
T
Tweak
Self-critique and the next step

See it in action

BAD
"Tell me about pneumonia."
GOOD
"Explain community-acquired pneumonia management for an MBBS exam."
BETTER
"Act as a pulmonologist. Explain CAP management for an MBBS final-year student as a 6-point answer with empirical antibiotic choices per Indian guidelines. Keep it under 150 words, exam-oriented."

Three kinds of prompt

Quick

40–80 words

One focused output in a few sentences. Each has a TRY IT WITH example.

Standard

100–200 words

One real task done properly: context, numbered task, rules, output, a pro tip and the next prompt to run.

Master

250–450 words

A full workflow on its own page, with when to use it, why it exists and how to get more out of it.

Tags you will see in the outputs

TagWhat to do
[CITE] · [CITATION NEEDED]Find a real source on PubMed and insert it yourself.
[VERIFY] · [ASSUMPTION — VERIFY FROM PUBLISHED DATA]Replace the number with a sourced figure before use.
[UNVERIFIED BACKGROUND]Treat as a lead to check, not a fact.

1Feasibility-first topic shortlist
Master
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident (Year 1)
Use itMonths 1 to 3 of your postgraduate course, before you commit to anything.
WhyMost theses fail at the topic, not the analysis. This prompt checks every idea against your real patient numbers, equipment and time before you fall in love with it.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a senior clinical research methodologist with over 20 years of experience supervising postgraduate theses in [YOUR SPECIALTY]. You have sat on an institutional ethics committee and you peer review for specialty journals. You are blunt about feasibility rather than encouraging.
My context
Specialty: [e.g. Respiratory Medicine]
Programme and year: [MD / MS / DNB / DM / MCh], year [1 / 2 / 3]
Institution type: [government medical college / tertiary private / district hospital]
Eligible patients I can realistically access: about [N] per month
Months available for recruitment and data collection: [X]
Funding: [none / departmental consumables / grant of amount Y]
Investigations and equipment I genuinely have: [list]
What I do NOT have: [e.g. flow cytometry, PET-CT, research nurse]
Statistical support: [none / department statistician / paid]
Clinical interests: [2 to 4]
Diseases with high burden in my setting: [list]
Task
Generate 8 candidate research questions I could design, complete, defend and publish inside my timeline. For each, give exactly:
1. Working title as it would appear on a thesis cover page.
2. PICOT: Population, Intervention or Exposure, Comparator, Outcome, Timeframe.
3. The gap in two sentences: what is established, what remains unanswered (describe evidence generically).
4. Study design, and one line on why not the obvious alternative.
5. Feasibility score out of 5, justified against my context above.
6. Indicative sample size with every assumption stated.
7. Recruitment timeline in months, assuming 30% of eligible patients are lost.
8. Primary outcome and the exact instrument, scale or cut-off.
9. The three most likely reasons it fails.
10. Honest publication ceiling: local, national indexed, or international.
Rules
Mark every number you need as [ASSUMPTION — VERIFY FROM PUBLISHED DATA] instead of inventing prevalence, effect sizes, trial names or citations, because my guide will ask where each came from. Drop any question needing resources I said I do not have. At least 2 of the 8 must survive if recruitment halves.
Output
A ranked list from most to least feasible, then your top pick with reasoning.
Tweak
Finish with AVOID THESE: three topic directions in my field that look attractive to postgraduates but routinely fail, and why.

How to get more out of it

  • Run it twice, once with your real patient numbers and once with half. Any topic that survives both is the safe one.
  • Take the top three to your guide as a one-page comparison, not a chatbot transcript.
  • Read AVOID THESE before you fall in love with an idea.

2Five Indian abstracts → six thesis topics
Master
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident
Use itAfter you have saved 5 recent abstracts from Indian centres in your field (Semantic Scholar or PubMed, last 3 years).
WhyTopics generated from memory repeat what already exists. Topics generated from real recent Indian abstracts start from what your peers have actually published, and from what they left out.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a research mentor at an Indian medical college who has guided over 50 postgraduate theses to completion. You read abstracts like a reviewer: you look for what was not measured, not studied, or not studied here.
My context
Specialty and programme: [e.g. MD Pulmonary Medicine, year 1]
Setting: [government medical college / private tertiary / district]
Limits: 2-year thesis window, [limited / moderate] lab infrastructure, about [N] eligible patients per month.
[PASTE ABSTRACT 1]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 2]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 3]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 4]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 5]
Task
1. For each abstract, write one line on what it studied and one line on what it did not address (population, outcome, setting, follow-up or design).
2. From these, identify 3 research gaps relevant to Indian patients.
3. For each gap, propose 2 thesis topics, each with a PICO question and a measurable clinical outcome.
4. For all 6 topics, give: best study design, rough sample size range, primary outcome measure, the main feasibility challenge in an Indian government hospital, and publication potential in an indexed journal.
5. Rank the 6 and recommend the top 3 to present to my guide.
Rules
Work only from the abstracts I pasted. If a gap needs knowledge beyond them, label it [UNVERIFIED BACKGROUND — CONFIRM ON PUBMED] rather than stating it as fact. Name no additional papers unless you can give a verified PMID or DOI.
Output
A short table per gap, then the ranked shortlist with one-paragraph reasoning for each of the top 3.
Tweak
End with the single question I should search on Consensus to check whether my top topic is already saturated.

How to get more out of it

  • If an abstract is from a different population (Western, paediatric), say so in the context line. It changes which gaps are real.
  • Keep the 5 abstracts. They become the first five studies in your Review of Literature.
  • If fewer than 3 real gaps appear, your search was too narrow. Search the gap, not the disease name.

3Socratic research-question workshop
Master
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident / MBBS Intern
Use itWhen you have a vague idea ("something on sepsis in the ICU") but no question.
WhyA good question is refined, not generated. This prompt makes the AI interview you, one question at a time, until the question is sharp enough to defend.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a clinical epidemiologist who runs research-methods workshops for postgraduates. You never hand over answers early. You ask probing questions, one at a time, and you push until the question is specific, measurable and feasible.
My context
My rough idea in one or two sentences: [e.g. something about delirium in elderly ICU patients]
Specialty and level: [e.g. MD Anaesthesiology, year 1]
Setting: [e.g. 20-bed mixed ICU, government medical college]
Time for data collection: [X months]
Task
Run a workshop with me in rounds:
1. Ask me one question at a time about population, exposure or intervention, comparator, outcome, timeframe and setting. Wait for my answer before asking the next.
2. After every 3 answers, show the current version of my research question and score it on FINER (Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant), 1 to 5 each, with one line per score.
3. Point out the weakest FINER element and ask the question that fixes it.
4. Stop when every FINER score is 4 or more, or after 5 rounds.
5. Then give the final question in PICOT format, a one-line hypothesis, and a draft working title.
Rules
Do not suggest a finished question before round 3, because the point is to make me think. If an answer I give makes the study unethical or impossible, say so directly and explain why. Keep every question you ask under 30 words.
Output
Conversational, one question per turn. Use a short FINER scorecard table at each checkpoint.
Tweak
At the end, list the 3 assumptions in my question that I must verify in the literature before my guide meeting.

How to get more out of it

  • Answer honestly, including "I don't know". The workshop is only as good as your answers.
  • Screenshot the final scorecard. It becomes slide 2 of your topic presentation.
  • Re-run the workshop for your second-choice idea and compare the final scores.

4Guide-meeting pitch pack for your top 3 topics
Master
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident
Use itThe day before you meet your guide or research committee to choose a topic.
WhyGuides respond far better to a student who arrives with three shortlisted topics, the evidence gap behind each, and an honest feasibility view. This prompt builds that one-page pitch.
COPY THE PROMPT BELOW
Persona
You are a head of department who has chaired thesis-topic committees for 15 years. You know which questions guides ask, which topics get approved, and which get sent back.
My context
Specialty and year: [e.g. MS General Surgery, year 1]
My guide's known interests: [list, or "unknown"]
Department case load relevant to these topics: [numbers per month]
Topic 1: [title + PICOT + why it matters]
Topic 2: [title + PICOT + why it matters]
Topic 3: [title + PICOT + why it matters]
Evidence I have for each gap (searches, Consensus result, abstracts): [PASTE NOTES]
Task
1. Build a one-page comparison table: title, design, sample size needed vs available, time to complete, cost, ethics complexity, novelty, publication ceiling.
2. Give each topic an overall recommendation: strong, possible, or weak, with one reason.
3. For each topic, write the 3 questions my guide is most likely to ask, with a short answer from my notes.
4. Write a 60-second verbal pitch for my first-choice topic.
5. List what I should bring to the meeting (search strings, hit counts, a feasibility estimate).
Rules
Base every feasibility judgement on the numbers I gave, not on general assumptions. Where my notes do not support a claim of novelty, write [GAP NOT YET VERIFIED] instead of asserting it, because a guide will test it. Keep my voice: a first-year resident, not a professor.
Output
The comparison table first, then the per-topic questions, the pitch script, and a checklist. Fits on two printed pages.
Tweak
Finish with the one weakness in my first-choice topic that I should raise before my guide does, and how to present it.

How to get more out of it

  • Print the table. A one-page comparison beats scrolling a phone in a guide's office.
  • If your guide rejects all three, ask which constraint mattered most, then re-run topic-feasibility with that constraint added.
  • Practise the 60-second pitch out loud twice.

5India gap finder: 15 topics from scratch
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident (Year 1)
Use itWhen you have no abstracts or ideas yet and need a starting list today.
Persona
You are a senior researcher in [SPECIALTY] at an Indian medical college, known for steering residents towards questions that matter to Indian patients rather than copying Western topics.
My context
I am doing my [MD / MS / DNB] in [SPECIALTY] at a [government medical college / private college] in India. I have limited lab infrastructure and a 2-year thesis window.
Task
Generate 15 potential thesis topics that:
1. Address conditions with a high burden in India (e.g. TB, diabetes, COPD, anaemia, dengue, snakebite, hypertension) relevant to my specialty.
2. Have limited published data from Indian populations.
3. Can be done as observational, cross-sectional or simple interventional studies.
4. Need no advanced laboratory infrastructure.

Describe gaps generically. Do not name papers or quote prevalence figures from memory; write [VERIFY] where a number would help, because every gap must be checked on PubMed before I pitch it.
Output

6Clinical hunch → PICOT question
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident / MBBS Intern
Use itWhen you have noticed something on the ward ("these patients seem to do worse") and want to turn it into a question.
Persona
You are a clinical research mentor who helps residents turn bedside observations into answerable research questions.
My context
What I noticed on the ward or in OPD: [describe the observation in 2–3 lines]
Specialty and setting: [e.g. Paediatrics, tertiary PICU]
Rough patient numbers per month: [N]
Task
1. Restate my observation as a clear clinical question.
2. Convert it into 3 alternative PICOT (or PECO) questions: one descriptive, one comparative, one prognostic.
3. For each, name the study design that answers it and the primary outcome.
4. Say which is most feasible for a postgraduate thesis and why.
Rules
Keep each question to one sentence. If my observation is more likely explained by bias or chance (e.g. referral pattern), say so first, because that changes the question.
Output
A table of the 3 PICOT versions, then a recommendation in 3 sentences.
Pro tipWrite your observation down the day you notice it. Specific details ("three patients this month on drug X") make much better questions than vague impressions.
Try it withElderly patients with hip fracture on our ward seem to develop delirium more often when surgery is delayed beyond 48 hours.

7FINER stress-test of my research question
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident
Use itBefore you submit your topic to the department or research committee.
Persona
You are a research committee member at an Indian medical college who scores postgraduate topics every term. You are fair but you do not pass weak questions.
My context
My research question: [paste in PICOT form]
Study design: [design]
Resources and patient numbers: [brief]
Time available: [months]
Task
1. Score my question on each FINER criterion (Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant) from 1 to 5, with two lines of justification each.
2. Identify the single weakest criterion.
3. Rewrite the question twice: once to fix the weakest criterion, once to make it simpler.
4. Say which of the three versions you would approve and why.
Rules
Judge novelty only on what you can reason from the question itself; where novelty depends on the literature, mark it [CHECK ON PUBMED] instead of guessing, because false novelty claims are caught at the viva.
Output
A FINER scorecard table, the two rewrites, and a verdict of 3 sentences or fewer.
Pro tipA score of 3 on "Novel" is fine for a thesis. A score below 3 on "Feasible" is not.
Try it withIs serum vitamin D level associated with exacerbation frequency in COPD patients at a tertiary hospital in north India?

8Is this topic already done to death?
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident
Use itAfter shortlisting, when you suspect your topic may already be heavily studied.
Persona
You are a systematic reviewer who can tell within minutes whether a research question is saturated, contested or open.
My context
My proposed question: [PICOT]
Search results I found (titles or abstracts from PubMed, Consensus or Perplexity): [PASTE TITLES / ABSTRACTS HERE]
Task
1. Sort the studies I pasted by design and setting (Indian vs international).
2. Judge the evidence state: saturated (consistent findings, many studies), contested (mixed findings), or open (few or no studies).
3. If saturated, suggest 3 angles that would still be original (new population, setting, outcome, follow-up, or measurement method).
4. If contested, identify what explains the disagreement and whether my study could resolve it.
Rules
Judge only from what I pasted. If the sample is too small to judge, say so and tell me which search to run next, because a verdict based on six titles is not a verdict.
Output
One-line verdict, then a short table of studies, then the angles or next search.
Pro tipConsensus's meter is a quick first pass. This prompt is the second pass on what it returns.
Try it withPaste 10 PubMed titles for "neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio sepsis mortality".

9Turn gaps into Consensus yes/no questions
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident
Use itBetween gap-finding (Perplexity) and topic selection, before you check evidence density.
Persona
You are a medical librarian who trains residents to query evidence engines such as Consensus effectively.
My context
Research gaps or problem areas I have noted: [PASTE 5–8 GAP AREAS HERE]
Population of interest: [e.g. Indian adults with type 2 diabetes]
Task
1. Convert each gap into one binary research question in the form "Does [factor] affect [outcome] in [population]?"
2. Give a second, narrower version of each question.
3. Tell me how to read the result: what a strong-agreement, mixed or insufficient-evidence result would mean for choosing my thesis.
Rules
Keep each question under 20 words and answerable with yes or no. Use my population exactly, because broad populations return Western data that does not apply to my setting.
Output
A numbered list: broad question, then narrow question, for each gap. Then a 3-row interpretation guide.
Pro tipKeep only the questions whose result is mixed or insufficient. Strong agreement means that topic is done.
Try it withGaps: sarcopenia in COPD, vitamin D in TB outcomes, early mobilisation in ICU.

10Topics I can do from existing hospital records
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident / DNB Trainee
Use itWhen prospective recruitment looks slow, or your thesis window is short.
Persona
You are a clinical researcher who has published multiple retrospective and record-based studies from Indian hospitals, and you know their pitfalls.
My context
Specialty: [SPECIALTY]
Records available: [e.g. ICU admission registers 2019–2024, discharge summaries, lab information system, OPD registers]
How complete and reliable these records are, in my honest view: [brief]
Approximate number of records: [N]
Task
1. Suggest 8 thesis questions answerable from these records.
2. For each, list the variables needed and whether they are routinely recorded.
3. Flag the main bias for each (missing data, misclassification, selection).
4. Say which questions most need an ethics waiver of consent, and what the IEC will want.
Rules
Prefer outcomes that are hard to misrecord (death, readmission, length of stay) over soft outcomes. Mark any question that needs data my records probably lack as [CHECK DATA AVAILABILITY] rather than assuming it is there.
Output
A table: question, variables, bias risk (low / medium / high), IEC note.
Pro tipPull 20 random files before committing. If more than a quarter are missing your key variable, choose another question.
Try it withMedicine ward records of acute kidney injury, 2020–2024, about 900 admissions.

12Topics needing only routine investigations
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident
Use itWhen there is no funding and you can only use tests the hospital already does free or routinely.
Persona
You are a researcher who has built a publication record entirely on routine clinical data in low-resource Indian hospitals.
My context
Specialty: [SPECIALTY]
Routine tests available at no cost to patients: [e.g. CBC, LFT, RFT, ABG, chest X-ray, ECG, bedside USG]

13Validate an existing clinical score in my population
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident
Use itWhen you want a solid, publishable design with a clear outcome, and a well-known score exists in your field.
Persona
You are a clinical epidemiologist experienced in external validation of prognostic and diagnostic scores in Indian populations.
My context
Candidate score or rule: [e.g. qSOFA, CURB-65, Alvarado, Wells]
Population where it was developed (if known): [describe or "unsure"]
My population and setting: [describe]
Outcome the score predicts: [e.g. 28-day mortality]
Task
1. Explain why validating this score in my population is (or is not) a genuine gap.
2. Outline the validation design: inclusion criteria, timing of scoring, outcome ascertainment, blinding of assessors.
3. Name the performance measures to report (discrimination, calibration) and what each means clinically.
4. Give the sample-size logic for validation studies (events needed, not just total patients).
Rules
Do not claim the score has never been validated in India; write [CHECK EXISTING INDIAN VALIDATIONS ON PUBMED], because that search decides whether this topic is novel.
Output
Four short headed sections, under 400 words in total.
Pro tipValidation studies are strongest when the score is calculated before the outcome is known. Build that into your protocol.
Try it withCURB-65 for 30-day mortality in community-acquired pneumonia at a district hospital.

15Questionnaire-based topics without the KAP trap
Standard
1.1 Topic selection & research question · PG Resident / MBBS Intern
Use itWhen your topic will rely on a questionnaire or scale.
Persona

The ethics of AI in research
  • Disclose AI use as your institution and target journal require.
  • Don't submit AI text verbatim. Rewrite it in your own academic voice.
  • Verify every statistic, reference and data point against the primary source.
  • AI cannot replace your guide's approval, your IEC clearance or your clinical judgement.

One last thing.

These prompts will not make you a researcher. They will make you a faster one, and only if the judgement behind them is yours. The clinicians who do well in the next decade will not be the ones who use AI the most. They will be the ones who know exactly when to stop trusting it.

Use them. Check everything. Publish work you can defend.
