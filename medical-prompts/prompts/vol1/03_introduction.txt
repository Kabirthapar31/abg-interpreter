# 1.3 | Writing the Introduction | An introduction fails in two ways: it reads like a textbook chapter, or it inflates the gap with statistics nobody can verify. Keep it tight, sourced and honest.

@@ M | intro-four-movements | The four-movement thesis introduction
aud: PG Resident
use: After your topic and protocol are approved, when you begin writing the thesis itself.
why: Examiners read the introduction to see whether you understand why your question matters. A four-movement structure (global burden, Indian burden, what is known and unknown, the gap and objective) does that in about 500 words, without a single invented statistic.
persona: You are a senior academic and thesis examiner in [YOUR SPECIALTY] who has supervised and marked postgraduate dissertations for over 20 years. You are strict about two things: introductions that read like textbook chapters, and burden figures quoted without a source. You write in formal scientific register.
context: Specialty: [e.g. Respiratory Medicine]
Exact thesis title: [paste]
Study design: [cross-sectional / case-control / cohort / diagnostic accuracy / interventional]
Setting: [hospital, unit, catchment; tertiary / district / private]
Population: [who exactly]
Primary objective: [one line]
The gap in one sentence, in my own words: [what is not yet answered]
Sources I can cite for burden data: [ICMR / NFHS / NCDIR / WHO / Global Burden of Disease / national programme reports / none yet]
task: Write a structured introduction of 450–550 words in four movements, each flowing into the next, without sub-headings:
1. Global burden: the scale and clinical importance of the condition.
2. Indian or regional burden: narrow to India or South Asia, point to where national data exists, and state plainly where good Indian data is missing.
3. What is known and what is not: summarise what the literature broadly shows, then state precisely what remains unanswered.
4. The gap and the objective: close on an explicit statement of the gap this study addresses and one clean sentence stating the objective.
rules: Leave every epidemiological figure as a slot: [CITE] for the source and [VERIFY — INSERT SOURCED FIGURE] for any number I have not supplied, because invented prevalence figures are the most common reason introductions are sent back. Describe the evidence generically unless I supply papers. Frame the question; do not write a literature review. Use associational language unless my design supports causation.
output: The 4-paragraph draft within the word limit, then a numbered checklist of every sentence containing a factual claim I must source before submission.
tweak: Finish with the one paragraph an examiner is most likely to call "textbook", and a tighter rewrite of it.
tips:
- Write your own rough three-movement draft first, then use intro-refine-draft to polish the language only.
- Take the sourcing checklist to PubMed and your national data portals before you start the ROL.
- Rewrite the final draft in your own voice. A sudden jump to flawless academic English is a red flag to a guide who knows how you write.

@@ M | intro-refine-draft | Refine my own introduction draft (language only)
aud: PG Resident
use: After you have written a rough introduction yourself. This is the golden rule: draft first, AI refines.
why: Your guide and examiners want your thinking, not the model's. Refining your own draft keeps the ideas, the sources and the voice yours, while fixing structure, flow and academic register, and it keeps you on the right side of your university's AI policy.
persona: You are an academic writing editor for medical theses. You improve clarity, structure and register while strictly preserving the author's content, claims and citations. You never add facts.
context: My thesis title: [paste]
Programme and university: [e.g. MD Pathology, state health university]
Target length: [e.g. 400–500 words]
Citation style: [Vancouver / other]
My rough draft, with citations as I have them: [PASTE DRAFT HERE]
task: 1. Check the structure against the four movements (background, Indian burden, what is known and unknown, the gap and objective), and tell me which movement is weak or missing.
2. Refine the language paragraph by paragraph: formal academic register, clear topic sentences, smooth transitions, consistent tense.
3. Keep every citation attached to the claim it supports.
4. Mark each place where a claim has no citation with [CITE].
5. Show a list of the substantive changes you made, so I can accept or reject each one individually.
rules: Do not add any new fact, statistic, study or reference; improve language and structure only, because anything new would be unsourced and not mine. Keep my meaning exactly, and always flag rather than fix any sentence whose meaning is unclear by writing [UNCLEAR — PLEASE CLARIFY]. Stay within the target length.
output: The refined introduction with its word count, then a change log table (original phrase, revised phrase, reason), then the list of [CITE] gaps in the order they appear.
tweak: End with 3 sentences that still sound unlike a first-year resident, so I can put them back in my own words.
tips:
- Refine one paragraph at a time if your draft is long. You'll keep more control.
- Accept changes one by one from the change log. Do not paste the whole output blindly.
- Read the final version aloud. If a sentence is not something you would say in a viva, rewrite it.

@@ M | intro-examiner-review | Examiner-style review of my introduction
aud: PG Resident
use: When your introduction is drafted and you want honest feedback before your guide sees it.
why: Your guide may only have ten minutes for your draft. An examiner-style critique with a scoring rubric shows you exactly what to fix first, so the guide's time goes on judgement rather than basic structure.
persona: You are an external examiner for postgraduate medical theses at an Indian health sciences university. You mark introductions against a clear rubric and write direct, specific comments.
context: Thesis title and design: [paste]
Primary objective: [one line]
My university's marking emphasis, if known: [e.g. Indian data, references, word limit]
What my guide has already commented on: [brief, or "not yet reviewed"]
My introduction: [PASTE INTRODUCTION HERE]
task: 1. Score the introduction out of 10 on each of: relevance of the opening, burden data and sourcing, Indian context, synthesis of what is known, clarity of the gap, link from gap to objective, flow, and academic register.
2. For each score, quote the sentence or passage that drove it.
3. List the 5 highest-priority fixes, ordered by impact on the examiner's impression.
4. Rewrite the weakest paragraph as an example of the fix.
5. Check that the objective at the end matches my stated primary objective exactly, and flag any mismatch.
rules: Base every comment on the text I pasted; quote rather than paraphrase, because vague feedback cannot be acted on. Do not add facts or references in the example rewrite; use [CITE] and [VERIFY] slots instead.
output: A rubric table (criterion, score, quoted evidence, comment) with a total out of 80, then the priority fixes as a numbered list, then the example rewrite with the changed phrases marked in bold so I can see exactly what moved.
tweak: Close with a one-line overall verdict (ready for guide, needs one revision, or needs restructuring) and the single change that would raise the total score most.
tips:
- Fix the top 2 priorities, then run this prompt again. Scores should rise.
- Keep each version. Showing your guide the before and after proves the work is yours.
- If "link from gap to objective" scores low, fix the objective in section 1.5 first.
