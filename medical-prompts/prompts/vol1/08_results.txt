# 1.8 | Results, tables & figures | Results report what you found, in past tense, with no interpretation. Every number must match the output, and every table must earn its place.

@@ M | results-writer | Write the Results chapter from my analysis output
aud: PG Resident
use: After your analysis is complete and verified, before writing the discussion.
why: The results chapter is where transcription errors, interpretation creep and text that repeats the tables creep in. Writing it from your verified numbers in a fixed order (flow, baseline, primary, secondary) keeps it accurate, readable and defensible.
persona: You are a medical journal editor who writes and edits results sections. You report findings in past tense, in a logical order, with effect estimates and confidence intervals, and you never interpret in the results.
context: Design and reporting guideline: [e.g. prospective cohort, STROBE]
Participant flow numbers (screened, eligible, enrolled, lost, analysed): [numbers]
Table 1 baseline characteristics (paste the table): [PASTE TABLE]
Primary outcome result with effect estimate, 95% CI and p: [paste exactly]
Secondary outcome results: [paste exactly]
Tables and figures I have, with numbers and titles: [list]
Target length: [e.g. 800–1,200 words for a thesis; 500–700 for a journal]
task: 1. Open with participant flow, referring to the flow diagram.
2. Describe baseline characteristics briefly, highlighting only clinically important features and referring to Table 1.
3. Report the primary outcome with its effect estimate, 95% CI and exact p value.
4. Report secondary outcomes in the order of the objectives.
5. Report any pre-specified subgroup or sensitivity analyses, labelled as such.
6. Refer to each table and figure at the right point, and highlight rather than repeat their contents.
7. After the draft, list every number used with its source (table or output), for cross-checking.
rules: Use only the numbers I pasted, reproduced exactly, because a single mistyped number undermines the credibility of the whole chapter. No interpretation, no comparison with literature and no causal words. Report non-significant results with their estimates and CIs, never as "no effect". Qualify "significant" as statistically significant.
output: The results text with sub-headings by objective, then the number-source table.
tweak: End with the 3 results an examiner will look at first, and whether my text presents each one clearly.
tips:
- Write results before the discussion. It stops you writing results to fit your story.
- Check every number in the text against its table. Mismatches are the commonest results error.
- If the text simply repeats a table, cut the text to the key finding and refer to the table.

@@ M | tables-figures-master | Plan every table and figure in my results
aud: PG Resident
use: After analysis and before writing, to decide how your results will be displayed.
why: Examiners and reviewers read tables and figures first. A planned set, where each item answers an objective and nothing is duplicated, makes results easy to follow. A random pile of SPSS outputs and pie charts makes them hard work.
persona: You are a scientific data-visualisation editor for a medical journal. You match each result to the display that shows it most honestly and clearly, and you follow journal conventions for tables and figures.
context: Objectives: [PASTE OBJECTIVES]
Results for each objective (brief, with the variable types): [PASTE SUMMARY]
Number of tables and figures allowed (thesis or journal): [e.g. no limit / 4 tables + 2 figures]
Software I'll use for figures: [Excel / GraphPad / R / SPSS]
My university's or journal's figure requirements, if any: [e.g. 300 dpi, greyscale, maximum width]
task: 1. Propose the complete set: which results go in tables, which in figures, and which only in text.
2. For each table: title, rows, columns, summary format, footnotes and abbreviations.
3. For each figure: chart type and why (bar with error bars, box plot, forest plot, Kaplan-Meier, ROC, scatter with fit line, flow diagram), axes, units, labels and legend.
4. Flag displays to avoid for my data (pie charts, 3D charts, dynamite plots for skewed data, dual axes).
5. Give step-by-step instructions to create each figure in my software.
6. Number everything in order of mention in the text.
7. Say which displays could go in an appendix instead of the main chapter.
rules: Choose displays that show the distribution or uncertainty (CIs, IQRs) rather than hiding it, because misleading displays are a frequent examiner criticism. Use placeholders instead of values.
output: A display plan table (number, type, objective served, content), then table templates, figure specifications, the "avoid" list and the software steps.
tweak: End with the single figure that best tells the story of my thesis, and why.
tips:
- One figure that shows your primary result clearly is worth more than ten decorative charts.
- Keep fonts, colours and decimal places consistent across all tables and figures.
- Make every table understandable on its own, with a full title and footnotes.

@@ M | results-audit | Consistency audit: text, tables, figures and abstract
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When the thesis or manuscript is drafted, before submission.
why: Inconsistent numbers between the abstract, text, tables and figures are among the most common reasons reviewers lose trust in a paper, and examiners spot them fast. A systematic audit catches them all at once.
persona: You are a meticulous statistical reviewer for a medical journal who cross-checks every number in a manuscript for consistency and plausibility.
context: Abstract: [PASTE ABSTRACT]
Results text: [PASTE RESULTS TEXT]
Tables (as text): [PASTE TABLES]
Figure legends and any numbers shown in figures: [PASTE LEGENDS]
Total N and group sizes: [numbers]
Missing data per key variable: [brief]
Version of the document and date: [e.g. draft 3, 12 May]
task: 1. Extract every number from each section and match it across sections.
2. Flag any number that differs between sections.
3. Check internal arithmetic: percentages match their numerators and denominators, subgroup counts sum to totals, means lie within their ranges, and CIs contain their point estimates.
4. Check that p values are consistent with the CIs (a 95% CI excluding the null should go with p < 0.05).
5. Check that the denominators change sensibly where there is missing data, and that the missing data is reported somewhere.
6. Check that every table and figure is cited in the text, in order.
7. Check that the abstract's conclusion matches the primary result as reported in the results text.
rules: Report discrepancies without deciding which value is right, because only the original source output can settle that. Do not alter any numbers yourself.
output: A discrepancy table (number, location A, location B, issue), then the arithmetic and consistency check results, then the citation-order check and the abstract check.
tweak: Finish with the 3 discrepancies most likely to be noticed by an examiner or reviewer, to fix first.
tips:
- Fix discrepancies from the original output, never by adjusting the text to match a table.
- Re-run this audit after every major revision. New inconsistencies appear with each edit.
- Ask a colleague to check 10 random numbers against the raw output as an independent check.

@@ S | primary-outcome-para | Report the primary outcome properly
aud: PG Resident
use: For the most important paragraph of your results.
persona: You are a medical statistician who writes primary-outcome paragraphs that are accurate and complete.
context: Primary objective: [paste]
Result: event counts or group summaries, the effect estimate with 95% CI, and exact p value: [paste exactly]
Adjusted result, if any: [paste]
task: 1. Write the primary-outcome paragraph: summaries in each group, the effect estimate with 95% CI and p value, and the adjusted estimate if applicable.
2. Refer to the relevant table or figure.
3. Write a one-sentence version for the abstract.
rules: Report the estimate and CI before the p value, and use past tense without interpretation, because readers need the size and precision of the effect, not just significance.
output: The paragraph, then the abstract sentence.
tip: Give absolute numbers as well as relative measures, e.g. "18/100 vs 30/100 (RR 0.60, 95% CI …)".
try: 28-day mortality 22% vs 31%, RR 0.71 (95% CI 0.50–0.99), p = 0.04.

@@ S | table-formatting | Format my table to journal standard
aud: PG Resident
use: When your tables are copied from software output and look messy.
persona: You are a journal production editor who formats tables to house style.
context: My table (as text): [PASTE TABLE]
Target: [thesis / journal name]
What the table is meant to show: [one line]

@@ S | strip-interpretation | Remove interpretation from my results
aud: PG Resident
use: When your guide says your results read like a discussion.
persona: You are a strict methods editor who keeps results and discussion separate.
context: My results text: [PASTE TEXT]

@@ S | negative-results | Report a non-significant result honestly
aud: PG Resident
use: When your primary or key result is not statistically significant.
persona: You are a research-integrity-minded statistician who reports null results clearly and without spin.
context: The result (estimate, 95% CI, p): [paste]
Sample size and whether the study was powered for this outcome: [brief]
task: 1. Write the result sentence neutrally with the estimate and CI.
2. Explain what the CI does and does not rule out.
3. List spin phrases to avoid (e.g. "trend towards", "marginally significant", "no effect").
4. Suggest how the discussion should handle it (and point to that section).
rules: Do not suggest any re-analysis to reach significance, because that is p-hacking.
output: The result sentence, the CI explanation, the avoid list, then the discussion pointer.
tip: A well-reported null result from a well-conducted study is publishable. Spin is what gets it rejected.
try: OR 1.35 (95% CI 0.88–2.07), p = 0.17 for diabetes and wound dehiscence.
