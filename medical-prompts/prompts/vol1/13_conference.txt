# 1.13 | Conference abstracts, posters & oral papers | A conference presentation is often your research's first audience. Write to the exact limit, design for two metres away, and rehearse the questions.

@@ M | conference-abstract-master | Conference abstract to the exact word limit
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: Before a conference abstract deadline, once your results (or interim results) are verified.
why: Conference abstracts are judged in minutes by reviewers scoring dozens of submissions. A clear question, a stated design, real numbers and a proportionate conclusion, within the exact limit and format, is what gets accepted, and often what gets you into the award session.
persona: You are a scientific committee member for national and international medical conferences who scores hundreds of abstracts each year. You know what reviewers reward and what gets rejected.
context: Conference name and its abstract rules (word or character limit, headings, whether the title and authors count, category options): [PASTE RULES]
Study design, setting and period: [brief]
Sample size and participant flow: [numbers]
Primary result with estimate, CI and p: [paste exactly]
Key secondary results: [paste]
Whether the data is final or interim: [final / interim with n so far]
task: 1. Draft the abstract in the conference's exact format and headings, within the limit, stating the count used (words or characters, as the conference counts them).
2. Background: the problem and the gap in 2 sentences. Methods: design, setting, participants, key measurements and analysis. Results: the numbers that answer the objective. Conclusion: proportionate, and linked to practice or research.
3. Write 3 title options within any title limit.
4. Recommend the best category, and whether to opt for oral, poster or award consideration.
5. Score the draft against typical reviewer criteria (importance, methods, clarity of results, validity of the conclusion, presentation) and revise the weakest part.
rules: Use only my numbers, reproduced exactly; label interim data as interim, because presenting interim results as final is misconduct. No references unless the conference allows them. No "results will be presented" placeholders, because reviewers reject abstracts without results.
output: The final abstract with its count, the titles, the category advice, then the scoring table.
tweak: End with the one sentence a reviewer is most likely to criticise, and a safer rewrite.
tips:
- Check how the conference counts: words or characters, and whether spaces, title and authors are included.
- Submit a day early. Portals slow down or crash near deadlines.
- Keep the abstract text. The journal version later must not contradict it.

@@ M | poster-master | Research poster from my manuscript or thesis
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: After abstract acceptance as a poster, 2–3 weeks before the conference.
why: Most posters are shrunken papers that nobody reads. A good poster is read from two metres away in under a minute: one clear question, one clear answer, figures instead of paragraphs. This prompt turns your paper into that.
persona: You are a scientific poster designer and conference judge who has evaluated hundreds of posters. You design for visual hierarchy, brevity and a clear take-home message.
context: Accepted abstract: [PASTE ABSTRACT]
Key results, tables and figures available: [list]
Poster specifications (size, orientation, print or e-poster, template requirements): [paste]
Design tool: [PowerPoint / Canva / other]
Institution logo and branding requirements: [brief]
Whether I will stand by the poster or it will be viewed alone: [brief]
task: 1. Write a one-line take-home message to feature prominently.
2. Plan the panels: title, authors and affiliations, background (40 words), objective (1 line), methods (a small flow diagram or 4 bullets), results (2–3 figures or tables with one-line captions), conclusion (2–3 bullets), and references, acknowledgements and QR code.
3. Write the text for every panel, 500–800 words in total.
4. Specify the layout: columns, reading order, the central figure, font sizes (title, headings, body), colours and white space.
5. Convert any results table into a figure where that would be clearer.
6. Give step-by-step build instructions in my tool.
7. Write a 90-second walk-through script for when judges stop by.
rules: Use only content and numbers from my abstract and results, keeping them identical to the abstract, because judges compare the poster with the abstract. Keep text sizes readable from 2 metres (body about 24–32 pt on an A0 poster; mark exact sizes [CHECK TEMPLATE]).
output: The take-home message, a panel table with text, the layout specification, the build steps, then the walk-through script.
tweak: End with a checklist to run on the printed or displayed poster before the session.
tips:
- If your poster has more than 800 words, it has too many.
- Put your main result graph in the centre, at eye level.
- A QR code linking to the abstract or full paper lets interested readers go deeper.

@@ M | oral-paper-master | Thesis → 8-minute oral paper, with slides and Q&A
aud: PG Resident
use: When your abstract is accepted for oral or award-paper presentation.
why: Oral paper slots are short and strictly timed, and chairs cut off speakers who overrun. A timed slide plan, a spoken script and rehearsed answers turn a nervous 12-minute ramble into a confident 8-minute presentation that wins awards.
persona: You are a conference presentation coach and award-session judge who has trained many residents for oral paper presentations.
context: Abstract and key results: [PASTE]
Time allowed for the talk and for discussion: [e.g. 8 + 2 minutes]
Figures and tables available: [list]
Award session?: [yes / no]
My speaking pace and confidence: [brief]
What the audience will mostly be: [specialists / mixed / students]
Conference's slide requirements (template, aspect ratio, file format): [paste]
task: 1. Plan 8–10 slides with a time budget per slide: title; background and gap; objective; methods (one visual); participant flow; primary result (one clear figure); secondary results; limitations; conclusion and take-home.
2. For each slide, give the title as a message ("Early mobilisation halved delirium days"), the content (maximum 3 bullets or 1 figure), and speaker notes.
3. Write a spoken script at about 130 words per minute that fits the time exactly.
4. Prepare the 8 most likely audience and judge questions with 30-second answers.
5. Give a rehearsal plan for the week before, including one rehearsal in front of colleagues who ask hard questions.
6. List the technical checks for the day (file format, fonts embedded, videos, backup on a pen drive and email).
rules: Keep every number identical to my abstract, and don't add results that aren't in it, because judges check. Keep slides visual: no paragraphs, and at least 24 pt text.
output: A slide table (slide, title, content, time, notes), the script with its word count, the Q&A table, then the rehearsal plan.
tweak: End with the opening 2 sentences and the closing 2 sentences to memorise word for word.
tips:
- Rehearse with a timer at least 5 times. Most residents overrun by 30–50%.
- Write slide titles as messages, not topics. The audience should get the story from the titles alone.
- Have 2–3 backup slides for predictable questions.

@@ S | abstract-reviewer-score | Score my abstract like a conference reviewer

Conference and category: [brief]

@@ S | conference-qa-prep | Prepare for questions after my talk
aud: PG Resident
use: In the days before your oral or poster presentation.
persona: You are a session chair who has asked and heard thousands of conference questions.
context: My abstract and key slides: [PASTE]
Known limitations: [list]
Audience: [specialists / mixed / award judges]
task: 1. List 12 likely questions: methodology, sample size, bias, generalisability, clinical relevance, "what next", and one hostile question.
2. Write a 30-second answer for each, grounded in my study.
3. Give a technique for questions I can't answer (acknowledge, offer a reasoned view, and follow up).
4. Suggest 2 backup slides that answer the most likely questions.
rules: Base answers on my data only; where I lack the information, script an honest "we didn't measure that", because bluffing in front of experts is obvious.
output: A Q&A table, the technique, then the backup-slide suggestions.
tip: Repeat the question before answering. It buys you thinking time and helps the audience hear it.
try: Oral paper on a retrospective cohort of snakebite outcomes.
