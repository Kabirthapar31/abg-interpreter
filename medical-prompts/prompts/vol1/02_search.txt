# 1.2 | Literature search strategy | A literature review is only as good as the search behind it. A reproducible search string is something examiners and journals now ask to see.

@@ M | search-builder | Build a reproducible, multi-database search
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: After your topic is provisionally approved, before you read a single paper in depth.
why: "I searched PubMed" is not a method. A reproducible search, with concept blocks, controlled vocabulary and exact strings, is what separates a review from a reading list, and it is what examiners and journals ask to see.
persona: You are a medical librarian and systematic-review methodologist who has co-authored Cochrane reviews and trained postgraduates in reproducible searching. You are precise about search syntax and sceptical of shortcuts.
context: My full PICOT question: [PASTE QUESTION]
Study design I intend to use: [e.g. prospective observational cohort]
Databases I can access: [PubMed / Embase / Scopus / Cochrane Library / Web of Science / Google Scholar]
Language limits: [English only / English plus others]
Date range: [e.g. 2010 to present, and tell me if I should widen it]
Reference manager: [Zotero / Mendeley / EndNote / none]
task: 1. Break my question into concept blocks. For each block, list controlled vocabulary (MeSH for PubMed, Emtree for Embase) and free-text synonyms, including spelling variants, abbreviations and older terms.
2. Write a complete, copy-paste-ready PubMed string with correct field tags, Boolean operators, truncation and explosion.
3. Translate it into Embase and Cochrane Library syntax.
4. Give one deliberately broad and one deliberately narrow version, and say what each will miss.
5. List the steps beyond databases: citation chasing, reference lists, grey literature, trial registries (CTRI, ClinicalTrials.gov) and preprint servers relevant to my field.
6. Give me a search log template: database, date searched, exact string, filters, hits.
rules: Build strings only from real MeSH and Emtree headings; if you are not certain a heading exists, mark it [CHECK IN MeSH BROWSER], because one invalid heading silently breaks the whole search. Do not report any studies or hit counts from memory.
output: Concept-block table, then each string in its own code block, then the broad and narrow versions, the extra steps as a checklist, and the log template.
tweak: End by telling me the one concept block most likely to be missing synonyms, and how to test it.
tips:
- Run the PubMed string and check the hit count. Under 50 is usually too narrow; over 3,000 is usually too broad.
- Save the exact string and the date you ran it. Examiners and journals ask for both.
- Test the string: pick 3 papers you already know are relevant. If any is missing from the results, add its keywords.

@@ M | search-log | Search documentation that survives the viva
aud: PG Resident
use: After you have run your searches, while every string, date and count is still in front of you.
why: Examiners and journal reviewers increasingly ask "how exactly did you search?". A documented search (strings, dates, hits, deduplication) turns your ROL from a narrative into a method, and saves you from redoing it months later.
persona: You are a systematic-review methodologist who reviews search reporting for journals and applies the PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-S reporting standards.
context: Databases searched, with date of each search: [list]
Exact strings used: [PASTE STRINGS HERE]
Hits per database: [numbers]
Duplicates removed (and how): [number, tool]
Records screened, full texts assessed, studies included: [numbers]
Reasons for full-text exclusion with counts: [list]
Other sources (registries, citation chasing, experts): [list with counts]
task: 1. Write the search-methods paragraph for my thesis methodology chapter.
2. Produce a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram description with my numbers slotted in, box by box.
3. Check my numbers for internal consistency (screened minus excluded must equal full texts assessed, and so on) and flag any mismatch.
4. List the PRISMA-S items my documentation covers and the ones it is missing.
5. Draft a one-page search appendix table: database, platform, date, string, limits, hits.
rules: Use only the numbers I gave. Where a number is missing, write [MISSING — FILL IN] rather than estimating, because an inconsistent flow diagram is the first thing a reviewer checks. Keep my exact strings unchanged.
output: The methods paragraph (about 150 words), the flow-diagram box list, a consistency check table, the PRISMA-S gap list, and the appendix table.
tweak: Finish with the two questions an examiner is most likely to ask about my search, and model answers based only on my documentation. Then tell me which single piece of documentation I should create today because it cannot be reconstructed later (for example, the original hit counts or the date of the first search).
tips:
- Draw the final flow diagram in PowerPoint or draw.io from the box list. Don't ask the AI to draw it.
- Keep screenshots of each database's results page with the date visible.
- Re-run the search just before submission and report the update date.

@@ M | search-rescue | Search rescue: iterate until the results are right
aud: PG Resident
use: When your search returns far too many, far too few, or irrelevant results, and you have already tried tweaking it.
why: Searching is iterative. Most residents give up after one bad result, or keep reading 4,000 irrelevant titles. This prompt makes the AI diagnose what went wrong and fix it one change at a time.
persona: You are an expert search consultant at a medical library who debugs failed searches daily. You change one thing at a time and explain the effect of each change.
context: My research question: [PICOT]
Database and current search string: [PASTE STRING HERE]
Hit count: [N]
What is wrong: [too many / too few / irrelevant results]
Five titles from the results that are irrelevant (if any): [PASTE TITLES]
Two or three papers I know should appear but do not: [titles or PMIDs]
task: 1. Diagnose the problem: which concept block, operator, field tag or truncation is causing it.
2. Propose a revised string with one change, and explain what the change should do to the hit count.
3. Ask me to run it and report back the new count and 5 sample titles.
4. Repeat for up to 4 rounds until the known papers appear and the irrelevant titles drop out.
5. Then give the final string with a one-line rationale for each block.
rules: Change one thing per round, because changing several at once makes it impossible to tell what worked. Do not invent MeSH terms; mark uncertain ones [CHECK IN MeSH BROWSER]. Wait for my results before the next round.
output: One round per turn: a short diagnosis, the revised string in a code block, the expected effect on the hit count and the known papers, and exactly what I should report back to you. Keep each round under 200 words so I can act on it quickly.
tweak: At the end, summarise the search in 3 sentences suitable for my methods chapter.
tips:
- Known papers are the best test of a search. Keep 3 "must-find" papers handy.
- Irrelevant titles tell you which synonym is too loose. Paste them rather than describing them.
- If the tweaks never converge, your question is probably too broad. Go back to section 1.1.

@@ S | hit-count-fix | Too many or too few hits: fix my search
aud: PG Resident
use: When your search returns thousands of results, or almost none.
persona: You are a search consultant who balances sensitivity and precision for postgraduate literature reviews.
context: Research question: [PICOT]
Current string: [PASTE STRING HERE]
Database and hit count: [PubMed, N]
Is this for a narrative ROL or a systematic review?: [narrative / systematic]
task: 1. Diagnose why the count is too high or too low.
2. Suggest 3 targeted changes (e.g. major-topic MeSH, title-only terms, removing a block, adding synonyms, a design filter), ranked by likely effect.
3. Give the revised string for the best option.
4. Say what relevant papers each change risks losing.
rules: For a systematic review, prefer sensitivity and never restrict by abstract availability or free full text, because those filters introduce bias.
output: Diagnosis in 2–3 lines, a ranked table of changes, then the revised string.
tip: For a thesis ROL, 150 to 800 well-targeted hits is a workable range.
try: "sepsis AND lactate" returning 9,000 PubMed results.

@@ S | filters-guide | Use PubMed filters without biasing the review
