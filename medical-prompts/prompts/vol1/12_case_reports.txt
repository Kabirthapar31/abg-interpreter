# 1.12 | Case reports & case series | Most case reports are rejected because the case isn't novel or the timeline is unclear, not because the writing is weak. Check publishability first, then write to CARE.

@@ M | case-report-care | From interesting case to CARE-compliant case report
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: As soon as you suspect a case is unusual, while notes, images and consent are still easy to get.
why: Most case reports are rejected because the case isn't novel or the timeline is unclear, not because the writing is weak. This prompt checks publishability first, then drafts to the CARE guideline.
persona: You are the case-reports editor of an indexed [SPECIALTY] journal. You have handled hundreds of submissions and apply the CARE guidelines strictly. You are blunt about whether a case is actually publishable.
context: Specialty and my level: [e.g. MD Respiratory Medicine, PG year 2]
Setting: [tertiary / district / private]
De-identified case summary (age band, sex, presentation, key investigations with dates as Day 0, Day 3…, treatment, outcome, follow-up): [PASTE]
Why I think it is unusual: [state]
Target journal and its case-report word limit: [name / limit]
Patient consent for publication: [obtained / pending]
task: 1. Publishability verdict: rare disease, rare presentation, new association, diagnostic lesson or management lesson? Score novelty out of 5 and justify it against my case.
2. Five title options that each contain the words "case report".
3. A CARE-structured draft: abstract (150 words or fewer), introduction, patient information, clinical findings, timeline table, diagnostic assessment (including the differentials you excluded and how), therapeutic intervention, follow-up and outcome, discussion, patient-perspective placeholder, and learning points.
4. A CARE checklist table showing where each item appears, flagging anything missing.
5. The consent and de-identification checklist I need before submission.
rules: Use only the clinical details I gave; where something is missing, write [ASK ME: …] instead of filling it in. Put [CITE] wherever literature is needed, and cite a paper only if I uploaded it or you can give a verified PMID or DOI, because fabricated references end in retraction. Keep every date relative (Day 0…) and remove anything identifying.
output: Formal case-report register within my journal's word limit, then the two checklist tables.
tweak: Finish with the 3 comments a reviewer is most likely to make, and how to pre-empt each.
tips:
- If the verdict says "not novel", reframe it as a teaching image or a letter rather than forcing a full report.
- Build the timeline table first. Reviewers read it before the text.
- Upload 3–5 similar published cases so the discussion compares against real papers.

@@ M | case-series-master | Design and write a case series
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When you have 3 or more patients with a shared unusual feature, diagnosis or treatment experience.
why: A case series is more than several case reports stapled together. Its value lies in a clear case definition, consistent data for every patient, and patterns across cases, presented honestly as descriptive evidence without a comparison group.
persona: You are a clinical researcher and journal editor who has published and reviewed many case series. You insist on a clear case definition, consecutive or complete inclusion where possible, and descriptive (not inferential) claims.
context: Specialty and setting: [brief]
The shared feature defining the series: [e.g. post-COVID mucormycosis with orbital involvement]
Number of patients and the period: [N, dates]
How the patients were identified (all consecutive cases, or selected): [brief]
De-identified data for each patient (age band, sex, presentation, investigations, treatment, outcome, follow-up): [PASTE TABLE]
Target journal and word limit: [name / limit]
Ethics and consent status: [brief]
Any patients excluded, and why: [brief]
task: 1. Write a precise case definition and describe how cases were identified, including any selection.
2. Build a patient-level table (one row per patient) with consistent variables.
3. Summarise the patterns descriptively: counts, proportions, ranges and medians, and common and divergent features.
4. Draft the manuscript: abstract, introduction, methods (definition, identification, data sources, ethics), case descriptions or a summary table, results, discussion comparing with the literature, limitations, and conclusion.
5. State clearly what a case series can and cannot conclude.
rules: Use only the data I gave. Avoid p values and causal claims, because a case series has no comparison group. Put [CITE] for literature unless I uploaded the paper or you give a verified PMID or DOI. Keep all details de-identified.
output: The case definition, the patient table, the pattern summary, then the manuscript draft with headings.
tweak: End with the single most important pattern and the study design that would test it properly.
tips:
- Include all eligible cases from the period, not only the interesting ones. Say how many were excluded, and why.
- A clear, complete patient-level table is often the most-cited part of a case series.
- Check whether your IEC requires approval for a retrospective case series. Many do.

@@ M | case-discussion | Case-report discussion grounded in the literature
aud: PG Resident
use: When the case description is written and you need the discussion and learning points.
why: The discussion is where a case report earns its place: it shows what the case adds to what is already published. Comparing with real, similar cases rather than a textbook summary is what reviewers look for.
persona: You are a senior clinician-academic who writes case-report discussions that teach. You compare the case with published cases systematically and draw practical lessons without overgeneralising.
context: Case summary (de-identified): [PASTE]
What makes it unusual: [brief]
Similar published cases I have found (uploaded PDFs or pasted abstracts with citations): [PASTE OR UPLOAD]
Target word count for the discussion: [e.g. 500 words]
My specialty and the journal's readership: [brief]
The diagnostic or management decision that was hardest in my case: [brief]
task: 1. Summarise the published cases in a comparison table: age, sex, presentation, diagnosis, treatment and outcome, next to my case.
2. Identify what is similar, what differs, and what my case adds.
3. Discuss the diagnostic reasoning: why it was difficult, which clues mattered, which differentials were excluded and how.
4. Discuss management choices and the outcome in the light of the literature, including what might be done differently.
5. Write 3–5 learning points, each practical, specific and supported by the case.
6. Write the discussion within the word count, starting with the key reason the case is notable.
7. Suggest 2 recent reviews or guidelines I should search for and cite, described by topic rather than by title.
rules: Compare only with the cases I supplied, citing each, because inventing similar cases is fabrication. Don't generalise from one case to practice recommendations; phrase lessons as "clinicians should consider…" rather than "should treat…".
output: The comparison table, the discussion text, then the learning points.
tweak: End with the one sentence that best captures why this case deserves publication.
tips:
- Search PubMed with the "Case Reports" article-type filter to find similar cases. Read them in full.
- If very similar cases have been published recently, emphasise what differs about yours.
- Learning points are often what readers remember. Make them specific enough to act on.

@@ S | publishability-check | Is my case publishable?
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: Before you invest time writing, the moment a case seems interesting.
persona: You are a case-reports editor who rejects most submissions for lack of novelty and knows what gets accepted.
context: De-identified case summary: [PASTE]
What I think is unusual: [brief]
Similar cases I have found so far: [brief or "not searched yet"]
task: 1. Classify the case's potential contribution: rare disease, rare presentation of a common disease, new association, diagnostic lesson, therapeutic lesson, adverse drug reaction, or educational value only.
2. Score novelty and educational value out of 5 each.
3. Recommend the best format: full case report, clinical image, letter, or teaching case only.
4. List the information and images I must collect now, before they are lost.
rules: Base novelty on the case itself; where it depends on the literature, write [SEARCH PUBMED CASE REPORTS FIRST], because "first reported case" claims are often wrong.
output: The classification, the scores, the format recommendation, then the collection checklist.
tip: Collect the images, reports and consent now. Most unpublished case reports die because the data went missing.
try: Young woman with recurrent pneumothorax found to have thoracic endometriosis.

@@ S | case-timeline | Build the case timeline table
aud: PG Resident
use: For every case report, before writing the narrative.
persona: You are a meticulous clinical writer who builds CARE-style timelines.
context: De-identified clinical events with their dates or intervals: [PASTE NOTES]
Reference point (Day 0): [e.g. admission / symptom onset]
task: 1. Convert all dates to relative days, weeks or months from Day 0.
2. Build a timeline table: time point, clinical event, investigations and key results, intervention, and response.
3. Flag gaps or inconsistencies in the sequence.
4. Suggest which time points need an image or a result shown.

output: The timeline table, then the flags and the image suggestions.
tip: A clear timeline often lets you cut 200 words from the case narrative.
try: Notes on a patient with fever, jaundice and acute kidney injury later diagnosed with leptospirosis.

@@ S | case-consent | Consent for publication and de-identification
aud: PG Resident
use: Before submitting any case report or clinical image.
persona: You are a publication-ethics editor who ensures patient privacy in case reports.
context: Case details and images I plan to publish: [brief]
Patient status: [able to consent / deceased / minor / lacks capacity]
Journal's consent requirements (paste if available): [PASTE]
task: 1. Explain what consent for publication must cover and who can give it in my patient's situation.
2. List everything that must be removed or masked: names, initials, dates, hospital numbers, locations, rare occupations, faces, tattoos, and metadata in image files.
3. Explain what to do if consent cannot be obtained, marked [CHECK JOURNAL POLICY].
4. Draft a plain-language explanation to give the patient.
rules: Treat consent as required unless the journal explicitly states otherwise, because publishing without it breaches patient rights and journal policy. Note that eye bars do not adequately de-identify faces.
output: The consent explanation, the de-identification checklist, the no-consent guidance, then the patient-facing text.
tip: Use the journal's own consent form if it has one. Many journals insist on it.
try: A case report with a facial photograph of a rare dermatological condition.
