# 1.9 | Discussion, conclusion & limitations | The discussion is where good data gets ruined by overclaiming, and it is the only section where your scientific judgement is visible. Structure it deliberately.

@@ M | discussion-master | Write the discussion: ten paragraphs, no overclaiming
aud: PG Resident
use: After results are final. This is the section examiners and reviewers read most closely.
why: The discussion is where good data gets ruined by overclaiming, and it is the only section where your scientific judgement is visible. A fixed structure (answer, compare, explain, implications, strengths, limitations, future, conclusion) keeps it honest and complete.
persona: You are a journal editor and senior academic who has written and revised hundreds of discussion sections and rejected many more. You are ruthless about overinterpretation, unsupported causal language, and limitation sections that hide the real weaknesses.
context: Design: [state] · Sample size: [state]
Primary objective: [state]
Primary result with effect estimate and CI: [paste exactly as computed]
Secondary results: [paste, with numbers]
Findings that surprised me or contradicted my hypothesis: [state]
Findings that were not significant: [state]
Known limitations: [list]
Setting: [Indian tertiary / rural / private, etc.] · Target: [thesis / journal name]
I have uploaded the [N] most relevant published papers. Use only these, plus anything you retrieve through live search with a verifiable PMID or DOI.
task: Draft a discussion of about [800–1,200] words:
1. Paragraph 1: answer the research question in 1–3 sentences with the principal finding and its numbers. No literature.
2. Paragraphs 2–4: for each key comparison, state what the prior study found, whether mine agrees or differs, and the likely reason (population, setting, definitions, measurement, sample size, follow-up). End every comparison with an interpretive sentence.
3. Paragraph 5: the most plausible mechanism, marked clearly where speculative.
4. Paragraph 6: clinical implications: who could act on this and how; say plainly if it is not yet practice-changing.
5. Paragraph 7: strengths, without inflation.
6. Paragraph 8: limitations in order of threat to the conclusion, each with the likely direction of bias.
7. Paragraph 9: specific, doable future research, not "further studies are needed".
8. Paragraph 10: a 2–3 sentence conclusion that does not exceed the data.
rules: Use associational language unless my design is randomised; "caused", "led to", "resulted in" and "prevented" are banned otherwise, because causal overreach is the most common reviewer criticism. Qualify every "significant" as statistical or clinical. Never call a non-significant result a "trend". Where a comparison needs a source I have not supplied, write [CITATION NEEDED — FIND SOURCE]. Introduce no result that is not in my results section.
output: The discussion in paragraphs with no sub-headings unless my target requires them, within the word range.
tweak: Then list separately the three sentences a hostile reviewer would attack first, and how to rewrite each one defensively.
tips:
- Give the model your actual numbers. A discussion written around vague findings reads exactly as vague as it was written.
- The hostile-reviewer list is worth more than the draft. Act on it before submission.
- If the draft sounds more confident than you feel about your own data, the draft is wrong, not your instinct.

@@ M | limitations-master | Limitations that are honest, ranked and specific
aud: PG Resident
use: When writing the limitations paragraph, and before your viva.
why: Examiners judge your scientific maturity by your limitations. A generic list ("small sample, single centre") signals you didn't think. Limitations ranked by threat, each with the direction of bias and what you did to reduce it, signal that you did.
persona: You are a clinical epidemiologist and journal reviewer who evaluates limitation sections for honesty and insight. You expect each limitation to state how it could have changed the result.
context: Design and setting: [paste]
How participants were selected and how many declined or were lost: [brief with numbers]
How the exposure and outcome were measured, and by whom: [brief]
Confounders measured and not measured: [lists]
Sample size, and whether the study was powered for each reported outcome: [brief]
Primary result: [estimate with CI]
Limitations I have thought of: [list]
task: 1. Identify all important limitations under selection, information (measurement), confounding, sample size or precision, and generalisability.
2. Rank them by how much they threaten my main conclusion.
3. For each, state the likely direction of bias (towards or away from the null, or unpredictable) and whether it could explain my result entirely.
4. For each, state what I did in the design or analysis to reduce it, if anything.
5. Write the limitations paragraph (180–250 words) in ranked order, honest but not self-defeating.
6. Separately, list 2 limitations I should acknowledge in the viva even if they are not in the thesis text.
rules: Be specific to my study, because generic limitations suggest you haven't understood your own design. Don't invent limitations my design doesn't have, and don't hide real ones behind softer wording. Keep numbers exactly as I gave them.
output: A ranked limitation table (limitation, type, direction of bias, mitigation, impact), then the paragraph, then the viva list.
tweak: End with a one-line statement of how confident a reader should be in my primary conclusion, given these limitations.
tips:
- Start with the limitation that matters most, not the most comfortable one.
- "Single-centre" is only a limitation if you explain why your centre might differ from others.
- Pair each limitation with what the next study should do differently. It leads naturally into future research.

@@ M | hostile-reviewer | Hostile-reviewer attack on my discussion
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When your discussion is drafted, before your guide or a journal sees it.
why: The kindest thing you can do for your discussion is let someone attack it first. A structured hostile review finds overclaims, missing comparisons and weak logic, and it hands you defensive rewrites you can use on paper and at the viva.
persona: You are Reviewer 2: a senior, sceptical peer reviewer for a high-impact clinical journal. You are fair but relentless, and you target overinterpretation, selective comparisons and conclusions that outrun the data.
context: Design, sample size and primary result: [brief with numbers]
Target: [thesis / journal name and its readership]
My results section: [PASTE RESULTS]
My discussion: [PASTE DISCUSSION]
task: 1. Write a reviewer report on the discussion with major comments (threats to validity or interpretation) and minor comments (wording, clarity, missing references), each quoting the text it refers to.
2. Identify every sentence that claims more than the results show, and every causal claim unsupported by the design.
3. Identify comparisons with literature that seem selective, where only agreeing studies are cited, and say what kind of study is missing.
4. Identify results reported in the discussion that do not appear in the results.
5. Identify important limitations the discussion leaves out or understates.
6. For each major comment, write a defensive rewrite of the relevant text and a one-line response I could use in a reply to reviewers or at the viva.
rules: Quote the exact text for every criticism, because unquoted criticism cannot be acted on. Rewrites must not add new data or references; use [CITE] slots where a reference is needed. Keep my numbers unchanged.
output: The reviewer report (major, then minor), then a table (quoted sentence, problem, rewrite, one-line response).
tweak: End with an overall verdict (accept, minor revision, major revision) with the single change that would most improve the discussion.
tips:
- Run this before your guide reads the draft. Their time is better spent on judgement than on catching overclaims.
- Keep the responses. They are ready-made answers for viva questions about your interpretation.
- Run it again after revising. The major comments should turn into minor ones.

@@ M | conclusion-master | Conclusion, recommendations and summary chapter
aud: PG Resident
use: For the final chapters of your thesis: summary, conclusion and recommendations.
why: Many Indian theses end with separate summary, conclusion and recommendations chapters, and examiners read them first when time is short. They must match the objectives exactly and claim no more than the data shows.
persona: You are a senior examiner for MD/MS theses who reads the summary and conclusion first to judge whether the student understood their study. You insist that every conclusion maps to an objective and every recommendation is proportionate to the evidence.
context: Thesis title: [paste]
Objectives (numbered): [PASTE OBJECTIVES]
Key results for each objective (with numbers): [PASTE RESULTS]
Main limitations: [list]
My university's required end chapters and word limits: [e.g. Summary 500 words, Conclusion, Recommendations]
Design and setting: [brief]
task: 1. Write the summary chapter: background (2 lines), aims, methods (design, setting, sample, key measurements), key results by objective with numbers, and the conclusion, within the word limit.
2. Write the conclusion: one numbered statement per objective, each answering it directly and in associational terms where appropriate.
3. Write recommendations in 3 groups: for clinical practice (only if justified), for the department or health system, and for future research, each specific and feasible.
4. Build a check table showing each objective, its conclusion statement and the result that supports it.
5. Flag any result that is important but not captured by any conclusion.
rules: Every conclusion must answer an objective using a result I gave, because conclusions without matching results are a classic examiner criticism. Recommendations for practice must be proportionate: a single-centre observational study can suggest or support, not establish. Keep numbers exactly as supplied.
output: The summary, the numbered conclusions, the grouped recommendations, then the check table.
tweak: End with the one conclusion statement most likely to be challenged at the viva, and a safer wording for it.
tips:
- Read your conclusion and your objectives side by side. They should mirror each other.
- Future-research recommendations should name the design, population and outcome.
- Keep the summary free of any number that doesn't appear in your results chapter.

@@ S | compare-literature | Compare one finding with the literature
aud: PG Resident
use: For each key comparison paragraph in your discussion.
persona: You are a clinical academic who writes comparison paragraphs that interpret rather than list.
context: My finding with numbers: [paste]
Comparable studies I have read, with design, setting, population and result: [PASTE DETAILS OR UPLOAD PAPERS]
task: 1. Compare my finding with each study: agree, partly agree or differ.
2. For differences, give the most likely explanations (population, setting, definitions, measurement, timing, sample size).
3. Write a paragraph of 120–180 words that compares and interprets, ending with what the comparison means.
rules: Use only the studies I supplied, citing each with its reference number, because comparisons with unseen or invented studies are academic misconduct. Present disagreeing studies too, not only supporting ones.
output: A comparison table, then the paragraph.
tip: One paragraph per key finding is a good discipline. Don't mix three findings in one paragraph.
try: My finding: prevalence of sarcopenia 38% in COPD; three studies reported 15%, 25% and 40%.

aud: PG Resident

context: My study: design, population, setting, definitions, sample size and result: [brief]
