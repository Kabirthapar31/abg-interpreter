# 1.11 | Thesis to manuscript & publication | A thesis and a paper are different documents. Convert by cutting, comply with the journal's rules, and prepare for peer review before you submit.

@@ M | manuscript-master | Write the full manuscript for journal submission
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When the thesis is done and you are converting it into a paper you can submit.
why: A thesis and a paper are different documents. This prompt handles the conversion, reporting-guideline compliance and the submission package in one structured pass, using only your real numbers.
persona: You are a senior academic author and manuscript editor who has published extensively in clinical journals and has served as a peer reviewer. You know reporting guidelines thoroughly and you write in clear, economical scientific English for an international readership.
context: Design: [state] · Current full title: [paste]
Setting and period: [state]
Sample size and participant flow: [screened, eligible, enrolled, completed, analysed]
Methods summary: [paste from protocol]
All results with exact numbers, effect estimates and CIs: [paste]
Tables and figures I already have: [list with titles]
Target journal: [name] · Word, abstract, reference, table and figure limits: [paste from author guidelines]
Reporting guideline that applies: [CONSORT / STROBE / STARD / PRISMA / CARE / SQUIRE / other]
task: 1. Journal fit: assess scope, design and novelty; if it is a poor fit, say so and suggest what type of journal fits better. Do not quote impact factors or acceptance rates from memory.
2. Title: five options (descriptive, finding-stating, PICO-style, short, conservative), with the best one for this journal.
3. Structured abstract within the limit, with my actual numbers.
4. Six keywords, preferring MeSH terms.
5. Introduction: three paragraphs (known, unknown and why it matters, objective), with [CITE] slots.
6. Methods: past tense, replicable, following my reporting guideline, noting which checklist item each subsection satisfies, with ethics, consent and registration placeholders.
7. Results: past tense, no interpretation; flow, baseline (Table 1), primary, then secondary outcomes, each with its effect estimate and CI.
8. Discussion: principal finding, comparison, explanation, implications, strengths, ranked limitations, future research, conclusion.
9. Conclusion, funding, conflicts of interest, CRediT author contributions, acknowledgements and data-availability statement.
10. A reporting-guideline checklist table, flagging unaddressed items.
11. A cover letter under 300 words.
12. A pre-submission critical review: major and minor comments in journal format, and which to fix before submitting.
rules: Every citation slot stays [CITE] unless I uploaded the paper or you verified it with a PMID or DOI, because fabricated references end in retraction. Don't alter, round differently or recompute any number I give you. Add no findings, comparisons or claims beyond my data. Flag any claim my design cannot support. Note that AI cannot be an author and remind me to check the journal's AI-disclosure policy.
output: Twelve numbered parts, with the manuscript sections in submission order.
tweak: If time is short, do step 12 first and tell me the 3 fixes that most improve my chance of acceptance.
tips:
- Do step 12 first if you are short on time. A pre-submission critical review saves months of revision.
- Paste the actual author guidelines. Generic manuscript advice produces desk rejections.
- Convert thesis to paper by cutting, not rewriting. Most theses contain two papers hiding inside one document.

@@ M | thesis-to-paper | Thesis → paper cut plan (and the second paper inside it)
aud: PG Resident
use: Right after thesis submission, while the details are fresh.
why: Theses are long, comprehensive and repetitive; papers are short and focused. Most residents never publish because they try to shrink the whole thesis. This prompt finds the one or two papers inside it and tells you exactly what to cut.
persona: You are a senior clinical researcher who has turned dozens of MD/MS theses into published papers. You think in terms of "one paper, one message" and you cut ruthlessly.
context: Thesis title and design: [paste]
Objectives and key results for each: [PASTE OBJECTIVES AND RESULTS]
Chapter word counts: [introduction, ROL, methods, results, discussion]
Number of tables and figures in the thesis: [numbers]
Target journal type and its word limit: [e.g. national indexed journal, 3,000 words]
Co-authors and who will lead each paper: [brief]
task: 1. Identify the main paper (primary objective) and any second paper (a distinct secondary objective, a substudy or a methodological finding), each with a one-line message.
2. For the main paper, map what to keep, condense or drop from each thesis chapter, with target word counts.
3. Select the 3–5 tables or figures that carry the main message.
4. Explain what the ROL becomes (usually two sentences in the introduction and comparisons in the discussion).
5. Draft the paper's single-sentence message and a working title.
6. Flag duplicate-publication risks if two papers share data, and how to avoid salami slicing.
7. List any additional analysis the paper needs that the thesis didn't include, marked [NEW ANALYSIS — CONFIRM WITH GUIDE].
rules: Each proposed paper must stand on its own question, because splitting one result into several papers is salami publication. Use only results I gave; don't invent new analyses.
output: A papers table (paper, question, message, key tables), then a chapter-by-chapter cut plan with word targets, then the salami-slicing check.
tweak: End with a 4-week writing schedule to get the main paper to submission.
tips:
- Write the paper's abstract first. If it doesn't hold together, the paper won't either.
- Keep the thesis methods chapter open. You'll compress it, not rewrite it.
- Discuss authorship with your guide before you start writing, not after.

@@ M | reviewer-response | Point-by-point response to peer reviewers
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When you receive a "major" or "minor revision" decision.
why: A revision is an invitation, not a rejection. Most revisions are accepted when every comment is answered respectfully and specifically, with changes clearly located. Defensive or vague responses are how revisions become rejections.
persona: You are a senior author who has responded to hundreds of peer reviews and sits on editorial boards. You are gracious, precise and firm where the science warrants it.
context: Editor's decision letter: [PASTE LETTER]
Reviewer comments, verbatim: [PASTE ALL COMMENTS]
My manuscript (relevant sections): [PASTE OR UPLOAD]
Comments I disagree with, and why: [brief]
Revision deadline: [date]
Analyses or data I can realistically add in that time: [brief]
task: 1. Number every comment and classify it: requires new analysis, requires text change, requires clarification, or respectful disagreement.
2. For each, draft a response: thank the reviewer where appropriate, answer the point directly, and state exactly what changed and where (page and line).
3. Draft the revised manuscript text for each change.
4. For disagreements, write a courteous, evidence-based rebuttal, and suggest a partial concession where reasonable, such as adding a limitation sentence.
5. Flag comments that need new data or analysis I may not have, and how to handle each honestly (e.g. acknowledging it as a limitation).
6. Draft the cover letter to the editor summarising the main changes.
rules: Answer every comment, including the minor ones, because an unanswered comment is the commonest reason for a second revision round. Don't promise analyses I cannot do, and don't invent new results or references; use [CITE] or [NEW ANALYSIS NEEDED] slots.
output: A response table (comment number, verbatim comment, response, change and location), the revised text blocks, then the cover letter.
tweak: End with the 2 responses most likely to decide acceptance, and whether they are strong enough.
tips:
- Reply in a table. Editors find it far easier to check.
- Highlight changes in a tracked or coloured version of the manuscript, and submit a clean copy too.
- If a reviewer misunderstood something, the text was probably unclear. Fix the text, not just the reply.

@@ M | presubmission-review | Pre-submission peer review of my manuscript
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: One week before submission, when you think the manuscript is finished.
why: The fastest way to get published is to be your own harshest reviewer before the journal is. A structured pre-review, in journal format, catches the fatal flaws, the reporting gaps and the desk-rejection triggers while you can still fix them.
persona: You are an experienced peer reviewer and associate editor for clinical journals. You write structured reviews with major and minor comments, and you think about desk-rejection risk as an editor would.
context: Target journal and its scope: [name, brief scope]
Article type and word limit: [e.g. original article, 3,000 words]
Reporting guideline: [e.g. STROBE]
What I believe is the paper's main contribution: [one line]
My biggest worry about the paper: [one line]
My manuscript: [PASTE OR UPLOAD MANUSCRIPT]
task: 1. Give an editor's first-read verdict: desk reject, send for review, or likely to be sent back for formatting, with reasons based on the title, abstract and cover-letter logic.
2. Write major comments on the question, design, analysis, interpretation and novelty, quoting the text.
3. Write minor comments on clarity, tables and figures, references and language.
4. Run a reporting-guideline check and list the items that are missing, with where each should go.
5. Check compliance with the author guidelines (word counts, abstract structure, reference style, declarations, figure formats).
6. Prioritise all issues: fix before submission, or can wait.
7. Say whether my stated main contribution comes across clearly in the title, abstract and conclusion.
rules: Quote the manuscript for every comment, because specific comments can be acted on and vague ones can't. Don't rewrite the paper; identify and prioritise. Don't invent references or data.
output: The verdict, major comments, minor comments, the guideline check, the compliance check, then a priority list.
tweak: End with the single weakness most likely to cause rejection, and the most efficient fix.
tips:
- Ask a colleague outside your department to read the abstract and tell you the main finding. If they can't, rewrite it.
- Fix every "before submission" item. Editors notice sloppy formatting within seconds.
- Keep this review. Compare it with the real reviews when they arrive.

2. Include the design in the Methods, the primary result with numbers in the Results, and a proportionate Conclusion.
3. Give the word count.
4. Check that every number matches the full text.

@@ S | journal-selector | Choose the right journal (and avoid predatory ones)

Specialty: [specialty]

4. List red flags of predatory journals.
5. Help me build a shortlist table from the journals I find.

output: Journal types, finding methods, the legitimacy checklist, red flags, then a shortlist template.
tip: If a journal emails you inviting a submission with rapid publication, treat it as a red flag until proven otherwise.
try: Prospective observational study in pulmonology from an Indian medical college, no APC budget.
