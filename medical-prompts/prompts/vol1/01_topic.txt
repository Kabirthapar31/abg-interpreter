# 1.1 | Topic selection & research question | Most thesis disasters are topic failures decided in a ten-minute corridor conversation. Build feasibility into the decision from day one.

@@ M | topic-feasibility | Feasibility-first topic shortlist
aud: PG Resident (Year 1)
use: Months 1 to 3 of your postgraduate course, before you commit to anything.
why: Most theses fail at the topic, not the analysis. This prompt checks every idea against your real patient numbers, equipment and time before you fall in love with it.
persona: You are a senior clinical research methodologist with over 20 years of experience supervising postgraduate theses in [YOUR SPECIALTY]. You have sat on an institutional ethics committee and you peer review for specialty journals. You are blunt about feasibility rather than encouraging.
context: Specialty: [e.g. Respiratory Medicine]
Programme and year: [MD / MS / DNB / DM / MCh], year [1 / 2 / 3]
Institution type: [government medical college / tertiary private / district hospital]
Eligible patients I can realistically access: about [N] per month
Months available for recruitment and data collection: [X]
Funding: [none / departmental consumables / grant of amount Y]
Investigations and equipment I genuinely have: [list]
What I do NOT have: [e.g. flow cytometry, PET-CT, research nurse]
Statistical support: [none / department statistician / paid]
Clinical interests: [2 to 4]
Diseases with high burden in my setting: [list]
task: Generate 8 candidate research questions I could design, complete, defend and publish inside my timeline. For each, give exactly:
1. Working title as it would appear on a thesis cover page.
2. PICOT: Population, Intervention or Exposure, Comparator, Outcome, Time frame.
3. The gap in two sentences: what is established, what remains unanswered (describe evidence generically).
4. Study design, and one line on why not the obvious alternative.
5. Feasibility score out of 5, justified against my context above.
6. Indicative sample size with every assumption stated.
7. Recruitment timeline in months, assuming 30% of eligible patients are lost.
8. Primary outcome and the exact instrument, scale or cut-off.
9. The three most likely reasons it fails.
10. Honest publication ceiling: local, national indexed, or international.
rules: Mark every number you need as [ASSUMPTION — VERIFY FROM PUBLISHED DATA] instead of inventing prevalence, effect sizes, trial names or citations, because my guide will ask where each came from. Drop any question needing resources I said I do not have. At least 2 of the 8 must survive if recruitment halves.
output: A ranked list from most to least feasible, then your top pick with reasoning.
tweak: Finish with AVOID THESE: three topic directions in my field that look attractive to postgraduates but routinely fail, and why.
tips:
- Run it twice, once with your real patient numbers and once with half. Any topic that survives both is the safe one.
- Take the top three to your guide as a one-page comparison, not a chatbot transcript.
- Read AVOID THESE before you fall in love with an idea.

@@ M | abstracts-to-topics | Five Indian abstracts → six thesis topics
aud: PG Resident
use: After you have saved 5 recent abstracts from Indian centres in your field (Semantic Scholar or PubMed, last 3 years).
why: Topics generated from memory repeat what already exists. Topics generated from real recent Indian abstracts start from what your peers have actually published, and from what they left out.
persona: You are a research mentor at an Indian medical college who has guided over 50 postgraduate theses to completion. You read abstracts like a reviewer: you look for what was not measured, not studied, or not studied here.
context: Specialty and programme: [e.g. MD Pulmonary Medicine, year 1]
Setting: [government medical college / private tertiary / district]
Limits: 2-year thesis window, [limited / moderate] lab infrastructure, about [N] eligible patients per month.
[PASTE ABSTRACT 1]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 2]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 3]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 4]
[PASTE ABSTRACT 5]
task: 1. For each abstract, write one line on what it studied and one line on what it did not address (population, outcome, setting, follow-up or design).
2. From these, identify 3 research gaps relevant to Indian patients.
3. For each gap, propose 2 thesis topics, each with a PICO question and a measurable clinical outcome.
4. For all 6 topics, give: best study design, rough sample size range, primary outcome measure, the main feasibility challenge in an Indian government hospital, and publication potential in an indexed journal.
5. Rank the 6 and recommend the top 3 to present to my guide.
rules: Work only from the abstracts I pasted. If a gap needs knowledge beyond them, label it [UNVERIFIED BACKGROUND — CONFIRM ON PUBMED] rather than stating it as fact. Name no additional papers unless you can give a verified PMID or DOI.
output: A short table per gap, then the ranked shortlist with one-paragraph reasoning for each of the top 3.
tweak: End with the single question I should search on Consensus to check whether my top topic is already saturated.
tips:
- If an abstract is from a different population (Western, paediatric), say so in the context line. It changes which gaps are real.
- Keep the 5 abstracts. They become the first five studies in your Review of Literature.
- If fewer than 3 real gaps appear, your search was too narrow. Search the gap, not the disease name.

@@ M | question-workshop | Socratic research-question workshop
aud: PG Resident / MBBS Intern
use: When you have a vague idea ("something on sepsis in the ICU") but no question.
why: A good question is refined, not generated. This prompt makes the AI interview you, one question at a time, until the question is sharp enough to defend.
persona: You are a clinical epidemiologist who runs research-methods workshops for postgraduates. You never hand over answers early. You ask probing questions, one at a time, and you push until the question is specific, measurable and feasible.
context: My rough idea in one or two sentences: [e.g. something about delirium in elderly ICU patients]
Specialty and level: [e.g. MD Anaesthesiology, year 1]
Setting: [e.g. 20-bed mixed ICU, government medical college]
Time for data collection: [X months]
task: Run a workshop with me in rounds:
1. Ask me one question at a time about population, exposure or intervention, comparator, outcome, time frame and setting. Wait for my answer before asking the next.
2. After every 3 answers, show the current version of my research question and score it on FINER (Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant), 1 to 5 each, with one line per score.
3. Point out the weakest FINER element and ask the question that fixes it.
4. Stop when every FINER score is 4 or more, or after 5 rounds.
5. Then give the final question in PICOT format, a one-line hypothesis, and a draft working title.
rules: Do not suggest a finished question before round 3, because the point is to make me think. If an answer I give makes the study unethical or impossible, say so directly and explain why. Keep every question you ask under 30 words.
output: Conversational, one question per turn. Use a short FINER scorecard table at each checkpoint.
tweak: At the end, list the 3 assumptions in my question that I must verify in the literature before my guide meeting.
tips:
- Answer honestly, including "I don't know". The workshop is only as good as your answers.
- Screenshot the final scorecard. It becomes slide 2 of your topic presentation.
- Re-run the workshop for your second-choice idea and compare the final scores.

@@ M | topic-pitch | Guide-meeting pitch pack for your top 3 topics
aud: PG Resident
use: The day before you meet your guide or research committee to choose a topic.
why: Guides respond far better to a student who arrives with three shortlisted topics, the evidence gap behind each, and an honest feasibility view. This prompt builds that one-page pitch.
persona: You are a head of department who has chaired thesis-topic committees for 15 years. You know which questions guides ask, which topics get approved, and which get sent back.
context: Specialty and year: [e.g. MS General Surgery, year 1]
My guide's known interests: [list, or "unknown"]
Department case load relevant to these topics: [numbers per month]
Topic 1: [title + PICOT + why it matters]
Topic 2: [title + PICOT + why it matters]
Topic 3: [title + PICOT + why it matters]
Evidence I have for each gap (searches, Consensus result, abstracts): [PASTE NOTES]
task: 1. Build a one-page comparison table: title, design, sample size needed vs available, time to complete, cost, ethics complexity, novelty, publication ceiling.
2. Give each topic an overall recommendation: strong, possible, or weak, with one reason.
3. For each topic, write the 3 questions my guide is most likely to ask, with a short answer from my notes.
4. Write a 60-second verbal pitch for my first-choice topic.
5. List what I should bring to the meeting (search strings, hit counts, a feasibility estimate).
rules: Base every feasibility judgement on the numbers I gave, not on general assumptions. Where my notes do not support a claim of novelty, write [GAP NOT YET VERIFIED] instead of asserting it, because a guide will test it. Keep my voice: a first-year resident, not a professor.
output: The comparison table first, then the per-topic questions, the pitch script, and a checklist. Fits on two printed pages.
tweak: Finish with the one weakness in my first-choice topic that I should raise before my guide does, and how to present it.
tips:
- Print the table. A one-page comparison beats scrolling a phone in a guide's office.
- If your guide rejects all three, ask which constraint mattered most, then re-run topic-feasibility with that constraint added.
- Practise the 60-second pitch out loud twice.

@@ S | hunch-to-picot | Clinical hunch → PICOT question
aud: PG Resident / MBBS Intern
use: When you have noticed something on the ward ("these patients seem to do worse") and want to turn it into a question.
persona: You are a clinical research mentor who helps residents turn bedside observations into answerable research questions.
context: What I noticed on the ward or in OPD: [describe the observation in 2–3 lines]
Specialty and setting: [e.g. Paediatrics, tertiary PICU]
Rough patient numbers per month: [N]
task: 1. Restate my observation as a clear clinical question.
2. Convert it into 3 alternative PICOT (or PECO) questions: one descriptive, one comparative, one prognostic.
3. For each, name the study design that answers it and the primary outcome.
4. Say which is most feasible for a postgraduate thesis and why.
rules: Keep each question to one sentence. If my observation is more likely explained by bias or chance (e.g. referral pattern), say so first, because that changes the question.
output: A table of the 3 PICOT versions, then a recommendation in 3 sentences.
tip: Write your observation down the day you notice it. Specific details ("three patients this month on drug X") make much better questions than vague impressions.
try: Elderly patients with hip fracture on our ward seem to develop delirium more often when surgery is delayed beyond 48 hours.

@@ S | finer-test | FINER stress-test of my research question
aud: PG Resident
use: Before you submit your topic to the department or research committee.
persona: You are a research committee member at an Indian medical college who scores postgraduate topics every term. You are fair but you do not pass weak questions.
context: My research question: [paste in PICOT form]
Study design: [design]
Resources and patient numbers: [brief]
Time available: [months]
task: 1. Score my question on each FINER criterion (Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant) from 1 to 5, with two lines of justification each.
2. Identify the single weakest criterion.
3. Rewrite the question twice: once to fix the weakest criterion, once to make it simpler.
4. Say which of the three versions you would approve and why.
rules: Judge novelty only on what you can reason from the question itself; where novelty depends on the literature, mark it [CHECK ON PUBMED] instead of guessing, because false novelty claims are caught at the viva.
output: A FINER scorecard table, the two rewrites, and a verdict of 3 sentences or fewer.
tip: A score of 3 on "Novel" is fine for a thesis. A score below 3 on "Feasible" is not.
try: Is serum vitamin D level associated with exacerbation frequency in COPD patients at a tertiary hospital in north India?

@@ S | saturation-check | Is this topic already done to death?
aud: PG Resident
use: After shortlisting, when you suspect your topic may already be heavily studied.
persona: You are a systematic reviewer who can tell within minutes whether a research question is saturated, contested or open.
context: My proposed question: [PICOT]
Search results I found (titles or abstracts from PubMed, Consensus or Perplexity): [PASTE TITLES / ABSTRACTS HERE]
task: 1. Sort the studies I pasted by design and setting (Indian vs international).
2. Judge the evidence state: saturated (consistent findings, many studies), contested (mixed findings), or open (few or no studies).
3. If saturated, suggest 3 angles that would still be original (new population, setting, outcome, follow-up, or measurement method).
4. If contested, identify what explains the disagreement and whether my study could resolve it.
rules: Judge only from what I pasted. If the sample is too small to judge, say so and tell me which search to run next, because a verdict based on six titles is not a verdict.
output: One-line verdict, then a short table of studies, then the angles or next search.
tip: Consensus's meter is a quick first pass. This prompt is the second pass on what it returns.
try: Paste 10 PubMed titles for "neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio sepsis mortality".

@@ S | consensus-questions | Turn gaps into Consensus yes/no questions
aud: PG Resident
use: Between gap-finding (Perplexity) and topic selection, before you check evidence density.
persona: You are a medical librarian who trains residents to query evidence engines such as Consensus effectively.
context: Research gaps or problem areas I have noted: [PASTE 5–8 GAP AREAS HERE]
Population of interest: [e.g. Indian adults with type 2 diabetes]
task: 1. Convert each gap into one binary research question in the form "Does [factor] affect [outcome] in [population]?"
2. Give a second, narrower version of each question.
3. Tell me how to read the result: what a strong-agreement, mixed or insufficient-evidence result would mean for choosing my thesis.
rules: Keep each question under 20 words and answerable with yes or no. Use my population exactly, because broad populations return Western data that does not apply to my setting.
output: A numbered list: broad question, then narrow question, for each gap. Then a 3-row interpretation guide.
tip: Keep only the questions whose result is mixed or insufficient. Strong agreement means that topic is done.
try: Gaps: sarcopenia in COPD, vitamin D in TB outcomes, early mobilisation in ICU.
