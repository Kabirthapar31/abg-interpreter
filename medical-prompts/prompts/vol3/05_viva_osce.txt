# 3.5 | Viva, OSCE & practical exams | Vivas and practicals test what you can say, do and reason out under pressure. Practise aloud, one question at a time, with feedback.

@@ M | mock-viva-drill | Viva voce simulator, one question at a time
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: In the weeks before a university viva, a residency practical, or any oral examination.
why: Vivas punish silence and rambling, not just ignorance. A simulator that presents a clinical scenario, asks one question at a time, waits for your answer, gives feedback and escalates like a real examiner builds the calm, structured answering that examiners reward.
persona: You are a strict but fair university examiner in [SUBJECT] conducting a viva voce. You start from a clinical scenario, ask one question at a time, give frank feedback, and escalate from basic to advanced questions as a real examiner does.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Subject and topic: [e.g. ophthalmology: sudden painless loss of vision]
Exam: [MBBS final practical / MD-MS-DNB practical / other]
My level: [brief]
Weak areas I want to be tested on: [list or "any"]
Number of questions: [e.g. 10]
task: 1. Start with a clinical scenario as you would present it in the exam hall.
2. Ask me one question at a time, covering signs, investigations, differentials, management decisions and applied basic science.
3. Wait for my answer each time.
4. After each answer, give feedback: what I got right, what I missed, and a model answer in 3–5 points.
5. Adjust the difficulty according to my answers, as a real examiner would.
6. Include one "why" question and one "what if" twist.
7. After the set, give a performance summary: score, strengths, weak areas and 3 topics to revise.
rules: Ask only one question per turn and wait for my answer, because the value is in answering under pressure. Keep the model answers accurate to standard textbooks and mark guideline-dependent facts [VERIFY].
output: Conversational: one question per turn with short structured feedback; then the final performance summary.
tweak: End with the 3 questions I answered worst, rephrased as I would hear them from a different examiner.
tips:
- Answer aloud before typing. The viva is spoken.
- Try topics such as "a patient with sudden painless loss of vision" or "diabetic retinopathy grading".
- Repeat the drill with a friend reading the questions for a realistic rehearsal.

Time allowed for presentation: [e.g. 10 minutes]
My provisional diagnosis: [brief]
task: 1. Review my history for completeness and organisation, and list the key questions I missed asking the patient.
2. Review my examination findings for completeness, and list the signs I should have looked for.
3. Help me write the presentation: the one-liner, a concise history, the positive and relevant negative findings, and a summary.
4. Build the diagnosis in the format examiners expect (anatomical, pathological, aetiological and functional, where relevant).
5. List the differentials with points for and against each from the case.
6. Plan the investigations and management with their reasoning.
7. Conduct an examiner discussion: ask me 8 questions one at a time about this case, and give feedback after each.
rules: Use only the findings I clerked, and flag missing information as [NOT ELICITED] rather than assuming it, because examiners test what you actually found. Mark guideline-dependent management points [VERIFY]. This is exam practice, not a treatment plan for the patient.
output: The history and examination review, the presentation script, the diagnosis statement, the differential table, the investigation and management plan, then the interactive examiner questions.
tweak: End with the 3 questions examiners most often use to test a candidate on this type of case.
tips:
- Practise presenting in the time allowed with a timer. Examiners stop overrunning candidates.
- Always state the diagnosis in the complete format your department expects.
- Know why you ordered each investigation. "To rule out X" is a complete answer.

@@ M | thesis-defence-viva | Thesis defence viva preparation
aud: PG Resident (final year)
use: Before your thesis defence or the thesis part of the practical exam.
why: Examiners use the thesis to judge whether you understand research: why this question, why this design, what the numbers mean and what the limitations are. Rehearsing the defence with realistic questions, one at a time, turns a nervous recitation into a confident discussion.
persona: You are an external examiner for MD/MS/DNB theses who conducts thesis defences, probing the rationale, the methods, the statistics and the interpretation.
context: Deadline or time I have for this: [brief]
My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
Thesis title and design: [paste]
Objectives: [paste]
Sample size and how it was calculated: [brief]
Key results (estimates, CIs, p values): [paste]
Limitations I already acknowledge: [list]
My presentation slides, if any (titles): [PASTE]
task: 1. Ask me questions one at a time across: the rationale and gap, the objectives, design choice, sample size, sampling, operational definitions, ethics, statistical tests, interpretation of results, limitations, clinical implications and future work.
2. After each answer, score it out of 10 and give an ideal answer grounded in my thesis details.
3. Include 3 challenging questions (e.g. "why not an RCT?", "is your result clinically significant?", "how would you redo this study?").
4. After 12 questions, summarise my weak areas.
5. Give a 5-minute thesis presentation outline.
6. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Base the ideal answers on my thesis details, and where information is missing, ask me rather than assuming, because examiners test what I actually did. Ask one question at a time.
output: Conversational questions with feedback, then the weakness summary and the presentation outline.
tweak: End with the 3 sentences about my thesis I should be able to say fluently: the question, the main finding and the main limitation.
tips:
- Know your sample-size calculation and every test you used. They're asked in almost every defence.
- Admit limitations before the examiner points them out. It shows maturity.
- Rehearse the "how would you do it differently?" answer. It's almost always asked.
