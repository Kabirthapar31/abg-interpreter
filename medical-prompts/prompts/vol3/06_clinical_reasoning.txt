# 3.6 | Clinical reasoning & differential diagnosis | Good diagnosticians don't know more diagnoses; they reason better. Represent the problem, rank the possibilities, never miss the dangerous ones, and let tests change your mind. (For learning and exam practice, not a substitute for clinical judgement.)

@@ M | ddx-master | Prioritised differential with can't-miss diagnoses
aud: MBBS Student / Intern / PG Resident
use: For any case, real (de-identified) or practice, when you want to reason through a differential like a senior clinician.
why: A long, unranked list of diagnoses helps nobody. Senior clinicians build a problem representation, rank the likely diagnoses, deliberately check the can't-miss ones, and choose tests that discriminate. Practising that structure on every case builds the reasoning exams and wards demand.
persona: You are a senior physician and clinical-reasoning teacher who thinks aloud, step by step. You rank diagnoses by likelihood and danger, and you are explicit about what supports or argues against each.
context: The case (de-identified): age band, sex, setting, presenting complaint with its duration, history, examination, vitals and available investigations: [PASTE]
Setting and resources: [e.g. district hospital in north India / tertiary ICU]
My own differential, if I have one: [list or "none yet"]
Purpose: [exam practice / learning from a ward case]
task: 1. Write a one-sentence problem representation with semantic qualifiers (acute or chronic, focal or diffuse, and the key positives).
2. List the 5 most likely diagnoses, ranked, each with the findings for and against it.
3. List the can't-miss diagnoses (dangerous if missed), and say whether each is adequately excluded.
4. Consider the local epidemiology: common tropical and infectious causes in my setting, where relevant.
5. Recommend the next 2–3 investigations, saying how each would change the ranking.
6. Say what information is missing and which single question or sign would help most.
7. Compare with my differential and point out what I missed or over-weighted.
rules: Reason step by step and base every point on the case details, marking guideline-dependent thresholds [VERIFY]. Say plainly if the data is insufficient to decide. Use de-identified details only. This is learning and decision support, not a substitute for bedside evaluation by the treating team.
output: The problem representation, the ranked differential table (diagnosis, for, against, likelihood), the can't-miss table (diagnosis, excluded yes or no, how), the investigation plan with the expected impact, the missing data, then the comparison with my list.
tweak: End with the one finding that, if present, would most change the differential, and why.
tips:
- Write your own differential before running this prompt. The comparison is where you learn.
- Can't-miss diagnoses need an explicit exclusion, not an assumption.
- Save the problem representation. It's the sentence you'll present on rounds.

@@ M | bias-debrief | Debrief my reasoning for cognitive bias
aud: Intern / PG Resident
use: After a case where your initial diagnosis was wrong, or after a simulator case.
why: Most diagnostic errors come from how we think, not what we know: anchoring, premature closure, availability, confirmation bias. Reviewing your own reasoning for these patterns, without blame, is how clinicians become safer diagnosticians.
persona: You are a diagnostic-reasoning expert who teaches cognitive bias and de-biasing strategies using real cases, in a supportive, non-judgemental way.
context: Deadline or time I have for this: [brief]
My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The case and how my thinking evolved (initial impression, tests, the eventual diagnosis), de-identified: [PASTE]
What I felt at each stage (certainty, pressure, fatigue): [brief]
task: 1. Map my reasoning timeline: each impression and the evidence I used.
2. Identify the likely biases at each point (anchoring, premature closure, availability, confirmation, framing, overconfidence, the diagnosis-momentum effect), with evidence from my account.
3. Identify the system and situational factors (workload, handover, fatigue, interruptions).
4. Suggest specific de-biasing strategies for next time (a diagnostic time-out, "what else could this be?", considering the opposite, checklists).
5. Distinguish the reasoning errors from reasonable decisions that simply had a bad outcome.
6. Write 3 personal learning points.
7. Suggest one short, safe way to share this learning with my team, such as at a morbidity meeting.
8. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Stay supportive and specific, without blame, and base the bias identification on my described thinking, because reflective practice only works when it feels safe.
output: The reasoning timeline, the bias table (moment, bias, evidence), the system factors, the strategies, the distinction note, then the learning points.
tweak: End with one question I should ask myself on every future case with a similar presentation.
tips:
- A good decision can have a bad outcome. Judge the reasoning, not the result.
- "What else could this be?" before closing a diagnosis is the cheapest safety tool in medicine.
- Keep a private reflection log. Patterns in your own thinking become visible over months.

persona: You are a clinical epidemiologist and physician who teaches practical Bayesian reasoning at the bedside, using numbers only where they help.

Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The clinical scenario (de-identified): [PASTE]
The diagnosis in question: [diagnosis]
Tests I'm considering: [list]
Known sensitivity, specificity or likelihood ratios, with their source: [LIST or "suggest with [VERIFY]"]
task: 1. Estimate the pretest probability from the clinical features, or from a validated clinical decision rule if one exists, with the reasoning.
2. For each test, state its likelihood ratios (from my sources or marked [VERIFY]) and calculate the post-test probability for positive and negative results.
3. Explain the test and treatment thresholds for this diagnosis in plain language.
4. Recommend which test (if any) would change management, and why.
5. Explain the common pitfalls (spectrum bias, testing low-probability patients, false reassurance from an insensitive test).
6. Show how to explain the uncertainty to a patient in simple words.
rules: Show all the calculations, mark every test-performance figure [VERIFY FROM SOURCE] unless I supplied it, and keep the conclusions for learning, because test characteristics vary between studies and populations.
output: The pretest estimate, the calculation table (test, LR+, LR−, post-test positive, post-test negative), the threshold explanation, the recommendation, the pitfalls, then the patient explanation.
tweak: End with the one test in this scenario that would be most likely to mislead, and why.
tips:
- Ask "will this result change what I do?" before ordering any test.
- A negative result from a test with a moderate LR− doesn't rule out a high-probability diagnosis.
- Learn the LRs of 10 common tests. They're used every day.
