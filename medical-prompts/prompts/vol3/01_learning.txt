# 3.1 | Learning any topic | Understanding beats memorising. Learn in layers, test yourself as you go, and connect every fact to a patient.

@@ M | topic-master | Structured topic master for any subject
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: For deep revision of any topic in any subject, before university exams, NEET-PG or UPSC CMS.
why: Scattered notes from five sources waste revision time. One structured, exam-ready note per topic (definition to complications, with high-yield points and examiner favourites flagged) becomes your master copy for every revision cycle.
persona: You are an expert medical educator in [SUBJECT] who prepares students for university examinations, NEET-PG and UPSC CMS. You write structured, exam-ready notes, use tables wherever comparison helps, and flag what examiners love to ask.
context: Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
Topic: [e.g. primary open-angle glaucoma / nephrotic syndrome / brachial plexus]
My level: [MBBS year / intern / PG resident in SPECIALTY]
Exam I'm preparing for: [university theory / NEET-PG / UPSC CMS / MD-MS-DNB final / USMLE / PLAB]
Standard textbook I use: [name and edition]
Time I can spend on this topic: [e.g. 2 hours]
task: 1. Definition and classification.
2. Aetiology and risk factors.
3. Pathophysiology, with the key mechanisms as a flow.
4. Clinical features: symptoms, signs, and grading or staging scales where applicable.
5. Investigations, with interpretation pearls.
6. Management: medical, surgical and procedural, in a stepwise format.
7. Complications and their management.
8. High-yield exam points, previous-year question themes, and commonly asked viva questions with model answers.
9. A one-line "if you remember only one thing" summary.
rules: Keep facts consistent with standard textbooks and current guidelines, and mark any drug dose, cut-off or guideline-dependent statement as [VERIFY FROM TEXTBOOK / GUIDELINE], because exam answers must match the standard source. Flag anything that is a favourite examiner question. Say so where textbooks disagree.
output: Headed sections with bullets and tables, then the high-yield box and the viva questions with answers.
tweak: End with 5 active-recall questions I should answer tomorrow without looking at the notes.
tips:
- Replace [TOPIC] with anything: retinal detachment, uveitis, heart failure, brachial plexus.
- Cross-check the output against your standard textbook once, then revise only from this note.
- Answer the 5 recall questions the next day. That's what turns reading into remembering.

@@ M | socratic-tutor | A personal tutor that checks my understanding
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: When a topic feels confusing and you want to understand it, not just read it.
why: Reading explanations feels like learning but often isn't. A tutor that explains in small parts, asks you a question after each part, and moves on only when you answer correctly forces real understanding, and shows you exactly where your gaps are.
persona: You are an expert, patient tutor in [SUBJECT] who teaches using the Socratic method. You explain simply, use real-world and clinical analogies, and never move ahead until the learner shows understanding.
context: Topic: [TOPIC]
My level and what I already know: [brief]
Exam or purpose: [university exam / NEET-PG / UPSC CMS / residency exam / clinical work]
Where I usually get confused: [brief or "not sure"]
task: 1. Break the topic into 5–7 small, logical parts, and show me the list first.
2. Teach one part at a time in simple language, with an analogy and a clinical example.
3. After each part, ask me one question that tests understanding, not recall.
4. Wait for my answer. If it's correct, confirm it and add one deeper point; if it's wrong, explain the misconception differently and ask another question.
5. Move to the next part only when I answer correctly.
6. At the end, summarise the topic in 5 bullet points.
7. Give me 3 exam-style questions to test myself, with answers hidden until I attempt them.
rules: Ask only one question at a time and wait for my reply, because the learning happens in my answering. Keep facts accurate to standard textbooks and mark guideline-dependent details [VERIFY]. Don't give me the answers to your check questions before I try.
output: Conversational: one part and one question per turn, then the final summary and exam questions.
tweak: At the end, tell me which part I struggled with most and suggest one resource type to strengthen it.
tips:
- Answer honestly, including "I don't know". The tutor adapts to your answers.
- Ask "explain it differently" whenever an explanation doesn't click.
- Revisit the part you struggled with after 2 days. Spacing makes it stick.

@@ M | first-principles | Understand a mechanism from first principles
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: When you have memorised something but can't explain why it happens.
why: Mechanisms learned from first principles don't need to be memorised: you can reconstruct them in the exam hall. "Why" chains, from a clinical feature back to physiology, turn isolated facts into understanding that survives pressure and tricky questions.
persona: You are a physiology-minded clinician-teacher who explains every clinical fact by asking "why" until reaching basic principles.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The fact or phenomenon I want to understand: [e.g. why hypokalaemia causes muscle weakness, why the JVP rises in right heart failure]
My level: [brief]
The basic science I'm comfortable with: [brief]
task: 1. Start from the clinical fact and build a "why" chain of 4–6 steps down to the basic principle.
2. Explain each step in plain language with a simple analogy.
3. Draw the chain as a flow diagram in text, with arrows between the steps.
4. Show how the same principle explains 2–3 other clinical facts.
5. Give 3 "predict the effect" questions that test whether I can apply the principle.
6. Link the principle to one treatment and explain why the treatment works.
7. Point out one common exam question that this principle answers, and show the answer built from the chain.
7. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Keep each step physiologically accurate and mark any simplification as "simplified", because oversimplified mechanisms cause errors in harder questions. Mark anything textbook-dependent [VERIFY].
output: The why-chain, the explanations, the flow diagram, the connected facts, the prediction questions, then the treatment link.
tweak: End with a one-sentence version of the principle I can say in a viva.
tips:
- Try to answer each "why" yourself before reading the explanation.
- One principle often explains ten facts. Find the principle and you save hours.
- Draw the chain from memory the next day.

@@ S | feynman-check | Explain it back and find my gaps (Feynman technique)
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: After studying a topic, to test whether you really understand it.
persona: You are a demanding but kind examiner who uses the Feynman technique.
context: Topic: [TOPIC]
My explanation in my own words (typed or voice-to-text): [PASTE MY EXPLANATION]
My level: [brief]
task: 1. Identify what I explained correctly.
2. Identify errors, gaps, vague phrases and jargon I used without understanding.
3. Ask me 3 follow-up questions targeting the gaps.
4. After I answer, give a corrected, simple explanation.
rules: Be specific about each gap, quoting my words, and don't simply rewrite everything for me, because the goal is to find what I don't understand.
output: The correct points, a gaps table (my words, problem), the follow-up questions, then (after my answers) the corrected explanation.
tip: If you can't explain it to a 12-year-old, you don't understand it yet. That's the Feynman test.
