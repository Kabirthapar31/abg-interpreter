# 3.7 | Memory aids & high-yield summaries | What you can recall on exam day matters more than what you once read. Turn every topic into flashcards, mnemonics and one-page sheets, and review them on a schedule.

@@ M | flashcard-maker | High-yield flashcards for Anki or Notion
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: After finishing a chapter or topic, to turn it into cards you can review for months.
why: Active recall and spaced repetition are the two best-evidenced study techniques. Well-made flashcards (one fact each, short, testing understanding, not recognition) make both easy. Poor cards (long, vague, copied paragraphs) waste review time and are soon abandoned.
persona: You are a medical educator and spaced-repetition expert who writes crisp, exam-focused flashcards for [SUBJECT], in the style of the best Anki decks.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Ophthalmology, year 1]
Subject and topic: [e.g. ophthalmology: glaucoma]
Exam I am preparing for: [NEET-PG / UPSC CMS / MD-MS-DNB university exam / (optional) USMLE, PLAB, MRCP]
My notes or textbook section, if I want cards from them: [PASTE, or "use standard textbooks"]
The app I will use: [Anki / Notion / RemNote / paper]
Number of cards: [e.g. 20]
task: 1. Write [20] high-yield flashcards on the topic in the format "Q: … | A: …", each answer 2 lines at most.
2. Cover a mix of eponymous signs, drug doses, landmark studies, grading and staging scales, mnemonics, and "what would you do next?" clinical questions.
3. Keep one fact per card; split any card that tests two things.
4. Add 5 cards on the most commonly confused facts in this topic (look-alike drugs, signs, grades or values), each contrasting the two.
5. Tag each card with a difficulty (easy, medium, hard) and a sub-topic, so I can filter them in my app.
6. Format the whole set so I can paste or import it directly into [Anki / Notion] (one card per line, with a separator the app accepts).
7. Mark any dose, cut-off or statistic [VERIFY], and give the source textbook or guideline where it matters [CITE].
rules: Write questions that make me recall, not recognise; do not write "true or false" or yes/no cards, because they test recognition. Keep the answers short, because long answers get skipped in review. Do not invent landmark trials or numbers; write [NOT REPORTED] if you are unsure, because a wrong card is memorised for years.
output: The cards as an importable list ("Q: … | A: … | tags"), then the 5 confused-facts cards, then a one-line note on anything I should verify.
tweak: End with 3 cloze-deletion versions of the hardest cards ("The drug of choice for {{c1::…}} is …").
tips:
- Make cards the same day you study the topic. Memory is freshest then.
- Review daily in small sessions; 15 minutes of reviews beats a 3-hour weekend cram.
- Delete or rewrite any card you keep failing. It is usually badly written.
- Suspend cards that are no longer exam-relevant rather than letting them clog your queue.

@@ M | high-yield-sheet | One-page high-yield summary of any topic
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: For final revision, when you need a whole topic on one page.
why: In the last weeks before an exam, you cannot re-read textbooks. A single page that holds the definitions, classification, key numbers, drugs of choice, investigations of choice and classic exam traps lets you revise a topic in 10 minutes and many topics in a day.
persona: You are a top NEET-PG and university-exam faculty member who writes famously concise, high-yield revision sheets.
context: Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Subject and topic: [e.g. medicine: rheumatic fever]
Exam: [NEET-PG / UPSC CMS / MD-MS-DNB university exam / (optional) USMLE, PLAB, MRCP]
My notes or previous-year questions on this topic, if any: [PASTE, or "none"]
What I keep forgetting in this topic: [brief, or "not sure"]
task: 1. Write a one-page summary in bullets under fixed headings: definition, epidemiology (Indian data where relevant), aetiology and risk factors, classification or criteria, clinical features, investigations (first and best), management (drug of choice, doses [VERIFY]), complications, and prognosis.
2. Put all key numbers (cut-offs, doses, percentages) in bold.
3. Add a "most-asked in exams" box of 5 facts, drawn from typical previous-year patterns.
4. Add a "classic traps" box: 3 facts that students commonly get wrong in MCQs.
5. Add one mnemonic for the list that is hardest to remember.
6. Keep it to one printed page (about 400–500 words).
rules: Keep every fact consistent with standard Indian textbooks and current guidelines, marking thresholds and doses [VERIFY] and guideline-specific facts [CITE], because revision sheets are trusted without re-checking. Use bullets and bold, not paragraphs, because the page must be scanned quickly.
output: The one-page sheet with the headings, the two boxes and the mnemonic.
tweak: End with 5 rapid-fire questions I should be able to answer after reading this sheet.
tips:
- Print it and add your own margin notes. Writing fixes memory.
- Make a sheet for each topic after the first reading, not the night before the exam.
- Keep all sheets for a subject in one folder, in textbook order, for fast final revision.

@@ M | mnemonic-builder | Custom mnemonics that actually stick
aud: MBBS Student / PG Resident
use: For long lists you must recall in order: causes, criteria, classifications, drug side effects.
why: Mnemonics work when they are vivid, meaningful and yours. Generic mnemonics are often forgotten because they carry no personal meaning. Building your own, with an image and a story, and testing them straight away, makes lists retrievable under exam pressure.
persona: You are a memory coach and medical educator who builds vivid, memorable mnemonics for medical lists.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
The list I need to remember: [PASTE the list, e.g. causes of clubbing]
Whether order matters: [yes/no]
My language preferences (English, Hindi or regional words, pop culture I know): [brief]
Mnemonics I already know for this list, if any: [brief, or "none"]
task: 1. Check the list for accuracy and completeness against standard textbooks; flag anything missing or doubtful [VERIFY].
2. Offer 3 different mnemonics: an acronym, a sentence mnemonic, and a visual story or image.
3. Use words and images that fit my language preferences and are vivid or funny, because odd images are remembered.
4. For each mnemonic, show exactly which letter or image maps to which item.
5. Say which mnemonic suits this list best and why.
6. Test me: after I choose one, ask me to recall the list from the mnemonic and correct my attempt.
7. Suggest when to review the mnemonic (for example, after 1 day, 3 days and 1 week).
rules: Map every letter or image to exactly one item, because ambiguous mnemonics cause errors in the exam. Keep the content accurate and mark doubtful items [VERIFY]. Keep it respectful: no offensive or crude humour, because I may share these with classmates.
output: The checked list, three mnemonics with their mappings, the recommendation, then the recall test after I choose.
tweak: End with a single line I can write on a sticky note to trigger the whole mnemonic.
tips:
- The sillier and more personal the image, the better it sticks.
- Say the mnemonic aloud and draw it once. Two senses beat one.
- Do not make mnemonics for short lists you can simply understand. Save them for arbitrary ones.

What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Subject: [e.g. pharmacology]
Exam and date: [brief]
Topics I am weakest in: [list]
Hours per day I have for this subject: [brief]
task: 1. List the 15 highest-yield topics in this subject for my exam, ranked by how often they appear in previous-year questions [VERIFY].
2. For each topic, give 3–5 one-liners I must know.
3. Add a table of the 10 most confusable pairs in the subject (for example, look-alike drugs or signs) with the one distinguishing feature.
4. Add a list of the key numbers and values in the subject (doses, cut-offs, normal values) marked [VERIFY].
5. Add a 20-question self-test covering the pack, with answers at the end.
6. Give a day-wise plan to cover the pack, prioritising my weak topics.
rules: Rank the topics by exam yield, not textbook length, because the last week is about marks per hour. Keep the facts accurate and mark every number [VERIFY]. Do not introduce rare topics, because new material in the last week displaces what I already know.
output: The ranked topic list with one-liners, the confusable-pairs table, the numbers list, the self-test with answers, then the day plan.
tweak: End with the 5 facts in this subject I should read on the morning of the exam.
tips:
- Take the self-test first; revise what you get wrong, then retake it.
- Sleep before the exam. Memory consolidation happens during sleep.
- Stop adding new sources in the final week. Trust your pack.
