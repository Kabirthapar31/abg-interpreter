# 2.2 | Journal club | A journal club is not a summary of a paper. It is a critical appraisal that ends with one question: should this change what we do for our patients?

@@ M | jc-master | Journal club presentation: appraisal to bottom line
aud: PG Resident
use: The week before you present, once your paper is chosen and you have the full text.
why: Most journal clubs retell the abstract. A strong one summarises the question, appraises it with a validated checklist, states the real strengths and limitations, and ends with a clear clinical bottom line for your patients. That is what faculty remember.
persona: You are a clinical epidemiologist who has moderated departmental journal clubs for 15 years and is known for spotting the flaw everyone else missed. You teach residents to appraise, not summarise.
context: My level and specialty: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience: [residents and faculty / mixed]
Time for presentation and discussion: [e.g. 20 + 15 minutes]
Checklist my department prefers: [CASP / JBI / CONSORT-based / none]
[PASTE FULL TEXT OF THE PAPER OR UPLOAD THE PDF]
task: 1. Summarise the study in 5 lines: question (PICO), design, setting, sample, and main result with numbers.
2. Explain why the study was done: the gap it addressed and the prior evidence, as described by the authors.
3. Appraise it with the appropriate checklist for its design, item by item, quoting the paper for each judgement.
4. Identify the 3 genuine strengths and the 3 limitations that most threaten the conclusion, with the likely direction of bias.
5. Interpret the results in clinical terms: absolute risk difference, NNT or NNH where applicable, and the precision of the estimates.
6. Judge applicability to Indian patients and our hospital's resources.
7. State the clinical bottom line in 2 sentences: would this change our practice, and why or why not?
8. Build a 12–15 slide outline with message titles, bullets and one-line speaker notes.
rules: Use only the paper I provided; where a detail is not reported, write [NOT REPORTED] rather than assuming, because the audience will check the paper. Calculate NNT only from numbers in the paper and show the arithmetic. Keep judgement and fact clearly separate.
output: The 5-line summary, the appraisal table (item, judgement, quote), the strengths and limitations, the clinical interpretation, the bottom line, then the slide outline.
tweak: End with the 3 questions faculty are most likely to ask me, with short answers grounded in the paper.
tips:
- Read the methods section twice. The flaw is almost always there, not in the discussion.
- Check the trial registry entry. Compare the registered primary outcome with the reported one.
- Practise the bottom line aloud. It is the sentence the head of department will remember.

@@ M | jc-paper-selection | Choose the right paper for journal club
aud: PG Resident
use: 2–3 weeks before your journal club, when you need to pick a paper.
why: The paper decides the journal club. A recent, practice-relevant paper with a clear design and a genuine debate makes a great session. An obscure or poorly designed paper makes for a long hour. This prompt helps you choose deliberately.
persona: You are a senior faculty member who coordinates the department's journal club and knows which papers generate good learning and discussion.
context: Specialty and my year: [brief]
Recent journal clubs in the department (so I don't repeat): [list]
Areas of practice that are debated in our unit: [list]
Candidate papers I have found (title, journal, year, design; paste abstracts if possible): [PASTE 3–6 CANDIDATES]
Departmental rules (e.g. published within the last 12 months, major journals only): [brief]
task: 1. Score each candidate on: relevance to our practice, recency, design quality, clarity of the question, potential for discussion, and teaching value for appraisal skills.
2. Flag any candidate that is a secondary analysis, a small pilot, or has an obvious fatal flaw that would make the session one-sided.
3. Recommend one paper and a backup, with reasons.
4. Suggest what to read alongside it: the accompanying editorial, the protocol, the trial registration and key prior studies (described by type, marked [FIND]).
5. Suggest the central debate question the journal club should resolve.
6. Explain how to confirm the paper's status (published version, corrections, retractions).
rules: Judge only from the abstracts and details I pasted, and don't claim facts about papers I didn't provide, because a wrong claim about a paper's findings will surface during the session. Prefer papers whose full text I can access legally.
output: A scoring table (paper, criterion scores, total, flags), the recommendation with a backup, the companion-reading list, then the debate question.
tweak: End with a one-line pitch I can send to the coordinator to get the paper approved.
tips:
- High-impact trials that challenge current practice make the best journal clubs.
- Check whether the paper has an accompanying editorial. It is a free second opinion.
- Avoid papers you can't obtain in full text. You can't appraise an abstract.

@@ M | jc-rct-appraisal | Deep appraisal of a randomised controlled trial
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When your journal-club paper is an RCT and you want to go beyond the checklist.
why: RCTs look robust, but their flaws are subtle: inadequate concealment, unblinded outcome assessment, outcome switching, post-hoc subgroups and inappropriate analysis populations. A structured deep appraisal finds what a quick read misses.
persona: You are a trial methodologist who appraises RCTs for guideline panels. You apply the Cochrane RoB 2 domains and the CONSORT checklist, and you explain statistics in clinical language.
context: The trial (upload the full text, and supplementary material if available): [UPLOAD]
Trial registration number and protocol, if available: [PASTE OR "not found"]
Clinical question I care about: [one line]
My comfort level with statistics: [beginner / intermediate / advanced]
task: 1. Randomisation and allocation concealment: how they were done, and whether they were adequate (quote the methods).
2. Blinding: who was blinded, and whether the primary outcome could be influenced by knowing the allocation.
3. Baseline balance and any imbalances that matter clinically.
4. Deviations and missing data: losses, crossovers, and ITT versus per-protocol analysis.
5. Outcomes: primary outcome definition, whether it is patient-important or surrogate, composite components, and whether it matches the registration.
6. Statistics: sample-size assumptions versus observed rates, the effect estimate with CI, NNT, and multiplicity.
7. Subgroups: pre-specified or not, interaction tests, and credibility.
8. An overall RoB 2 judgement per domain, and the certainty of the conclusion.
rules: Quote the paper for every judgement, and mark anything not reported as [NOT REPORTED], because unsupported criticism is easily dismissed in a journal club. Show the arithmetic for NNT and absolute differences. If I haven't provided the registry entry, say what to check there rather than assuming.
output: Eight headed sections, a RoB 2 domain table (domain, judgement, quote), then a one-paragraph verdict.
tweak: End with the single methodological issue most likely to change how the results should be interpreted.
tips:
- Look for the CONSORT flow diagram first. Losses and exclusions tell you a lot.
- A "significant" result on a surrogate outcome is not the same as a patient-important benefit.
- Compare the event rate in the control group with the one assumed in the sample-size calculation. If it's lower, the trial was probably underpowered.

@@ M | jc-observational-appraisal | Appraise a cohort or case-control study
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When your journal-club paper is observational.
why: Observational studies drive many practice changes, and many false beliefs. Confounding, selection and measurement bias can create associations that vanish in trials. Appraising them properly is one of the most important skills a clinician can have.
persona: You are an epidemiologist who teaches the appraisal of observational research, with STROBE for reporting and ROBINS-I or Newcastle-Ottawa for bias.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
The study (upload the full text): [UPLOAD]
Design as stated by the authors: [cohort / case-control / cross-sectional]
Clinical question I care about: [one line]
task: 1. Confirm the real design and the direction of inquiry, and whether the authors' label is correct.
2. Selection: source population, how participants were identified, and whether selection could relate to both exposure and outcome.
3. Measurement: how the exposure and outcome were measured, and whether misclassification is differential.
4. Confounding: which confounders were measured and adjusted for, which important ones are missing, and whether the adjustment set is sensible (no mediators or colliders).
5. Analysis: model choice, events per variable, handling of missing data, and sensitivity analyses.
6. Causality: apply the Bradford Hill considerations briefly to the main association.
7. Interpretation: whether the authors' language matches the design (association versus causation).
8. An overall risk-of-bias judgement and a bottom line for practice.
rules: Quote the paper for each judgement and mark anything missing as [NOT REPORTED], because journal-club audiences check claims against the text. Don't dismiss the study merely for being observational; judge how well it handled its own biases.
output: Eight headed sections, a bias table (domain, concern, likely direction), then a one-paragraph verdict.
tweak: End with the unmeasured confounder most likely to explain the result, and how a future study could address it.
tips:
- Draw the causal diagram for the main association. Missing confounders become obvious.
- Large effect sizes, dose-response relationships and consistency across studies strengthen a causal reading.
- Watch for "healthy user" effects in drug studies. They are a classic trap.

@@ M | jc-diagnostic-appraisal | Appraise a diagnostic accuracy study
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When your journal-club paper evaluates a test, score or imaging method.
why: Diagnostic studies are easily biased, through the wrong spectrum of patients, partial verification or unblinded reading, and the resulting accuracy figures often collapse in real practice. QUADAS-2 thinking protects you from adopting a test that won't perform.
persona: You are a diagnostic-research methodologist who applies QUADAS-2 and STARD, and who explains sensitivity, specificity and likelihood ratios in bedside terms.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
The study (upload the full text): [UPLOAD]
The clinical role I would use this test for: [replacement / triage / add-on]
Prevalence of the condition in my setting, if known: [estimate or "unknown"]
task: 1. Patient selection: consecutive or random, the case-control ("two-gate") design risk, and whether the spectrum matches my practice.
2. Index test: conduct, threshold (pre-specified or data-driven), and blinding to the reference standard.
3. Reference standard: whether it correctly classifies the condition, and blinding to the index test.
4. Flow and timing: the interval between tests, whether all patients received the same reference standard, and exclusions and indeterminate results.
5. Results: reconstruct the 2 × 2 table, then check the sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and likelihood ratios with 95% CIs.
6. Apply the results to my setting's prevalence: the post-test probabilities for a positive and a negative result.
7. A QUADAS-2 judgement per domain, with applicability concerns.
8. A bottom line: should we use this test, and for what role?
rules: Quote the paper for judgements, and show the arithmetic for recalculated values and post-test probabilities, because the audience may check them. Mark any numbers not reported as [NOT REPORTED] instead of inferring them.
output: Eight headed sections, the 2 × 2 table, the QUADAS-2 table, then the bottom line.
tweak: End with the patient group in whom this test would most likely mislead, and why.
tips:
- Likelihood ratios are more useful at the bedside than sensitivity and specificity. Teach them.
- If the threshold was chosen after seeing the data, the accuracy is optimistic.
- A test is only useful if it changes management. Ask what you would do differently with the result.

@@ M | jc-sr-appraisal | Appraise a systematic review or meta-analysis
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When your journal-club paper is a systematic review or meta-analysis.
why: Meta-analyses sit at the top of the evidence pyramid, but only if they are done well. Poor searches, mixing of incompatible studies, low-quality inputs and ignored heterogeneity can produce confident, wrong answers. AMSTAR 2 and GRADE thinking separate robust reviews from misleading ones.
persona: You are a systematic-review methodologist and GRADE-trained guideline developer who appraises reviews using AMSTAR 2 and explains forest plots clearly.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The review (upload the full text and supplements if available): [UPLOAD]
Clinical question I care about: [one line]
My comfort level with meta-analysis statistics: [beginner / intermediate / advanced]
task: 1. Question and protocol: a clear PICO, and whether it was registered (e.g. PROSPERO) before the review.
2. Search: databases, dates, grey literature, language limits, and whether it was comprehensive.
3. Selection and extraction: duplicate screening and extraction, and the list of excluded studies with reasons.
4. Risk of bias of the included studies: the tool used, and how the results were incorporated.
5. Synthesis: appropriateness of pooling, fixed versus random effects, heterogeneity (I², prediction interval), sensitivity and subgroup analyses.
6. Publication bias: funnel plot or tests, where appropriate.
7. Certainty of evidence: GRADE for the main outcomes, whether reported or as my estimate.
8. An AMSTAR 2 critical-domain judgement and a bottom line.
rules: Quote the paper for each judgement, and mark missing items as [NOT REPORTED], because reporting gaps are themselves findings. Explain the forest plot using the review's own numbers, without recomputing the pooled estimates.
output: Eight headed sections, the AMSTAR 2 table, a plain-language forest-plot explanation, then the bottom line.
tweak: End with whether a new trial is still needed on this question, and why.
tips:
- A meta-analysis of small, biased trials is still biased. Check the inputs before admiring the pooled result.
- High I² means "these studies disagree". Find out why before trusting the average.
- Look for the prediction interval. It shows the range of effects to expect in a new setting.

@@ M | jc-stats-decoder | Decode the statistics in my paper
aud: PG Resident
use: When the paper's statistics are beyond your comfort level and the journal club is soon.
why: Faculty often ask "what does this hazard ratio mean?" or "why this test?". Understanding the statistics in your own paper, in plain language, is the difference between a confident presenter and one who reads the numbers aloud.
persona: You are a biostatistician who teaches clinicians to read the statistics in published papers, in plain language first and technical language second.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The paper (upload, or paste the methods and results): [UPLOAD / PASTE]
Statistical terms I don't understand in it: [list]
My statistics background: [beginner / intermediate]
task: 1. List every statistical method used, and explain in one line why it was chosen for this data.
2. Explain each key result in plain language: the effect measure, its CI, the p value, and what they mean together.
3. Explain the terms I listed with an example from this paper.
4. Convert relative measures to absolute ones where the data allows (ARR, NNT), showing the arithmetic.
5. Point out any statistical choice a reviewer might question (e.g. multiplicity, per-protocol analysis, stepwise selection).
6. Write 3 "if asked, say this" sentences about the statistics for my journal club.
7. Suggest one simple visual to explain the main result.
8. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Use only the numbers in the paper, and if something can't be calculated from the reported data, say so rather than estimating, because recalculated numbers will be checked by the audience.
output: A methods table (method, purpose), the plain-language results, the glossary, the absolute measures, the questionable choices, then the scripted sentences.
tweak: End with the one statistics question I'm most likely to be asked, and the ideal answer.
tips:
- Always convert relative risk reductions into absolute numbers. It changes how impressive a result looks.
- If a CI crosses the line of no effect, say "inconclusive", not "no effect".
- Ask a statistician colleague to check your NNT arithmetic before the session.
