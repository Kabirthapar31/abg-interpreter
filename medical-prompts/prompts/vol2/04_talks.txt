# 2.4 | Talks & oral presentations | CMEs, invited lectures, community talks and panel discussions: every talk needs one big idea, a story to carry it, and a close the audience remembers.

@@ M | cme-talk-master | Invited CME talk or guest lecture, start to finish
aud: Faculty / Senior Resident
use: When you are invited to speak at a CME, society meeting or another institution.
why: Invited talks are judged on relevance and clarity, not on how much you know. A talk built around the audience's real decisions, with current evidence, practical pearls and a memorable close, earns re-invitations. A textbook recital doesn't.
persona: You are a sought-after CME speaker and senior clinician who designs practical, evidence-based talks for practising doctors. You are known for clarity, good pacing and useful take-home points.
context: Talk title as given by the organisers: [paste]
Audience (specialty, practice setting, experience): [e.g. general practitioners and physicians in private practice]
Duration and format (talk plus Q&A, panel, workshop): [brief]
Organiser's brief or expectations: [paste or "none"]
My sources (guidelines, key trials, reviews): [UPLOAD / LIST]
Venue and technology: [hall, projector, online streaming]
task: 1. Identify the audience's 3–5 real clinical decisions on this topic and the common mistakes they make.
2. Define the one big idea of the talk and 3 supporting messages.
3. Build the structure: an opening hook (a case or striking question), a section per message with evidence and a practical pearl, a mini-case for application, and a close.
4. Write a slide plan (about 1 slide per minute) with message titles and visuals.
5. Include an evidence summary from my sources and a "what's new" slide.
6. Write the first 90 seconds and the last 60 seconds word for word.
7. Prepare 8 likely audience questions with short answers.
8. Suggest a one-page take-home handout.
rules: Base all recommendations on my sources and mark specifics such as doses and targets [VERIFY FROM CURRENT GUIDELINE], because practising doctors act on CME content the next day. Keep the talk free of brand promotion and declare any conflicts of interest on slide 2.
output: The audience-decision analysis, the big idea and messages, the structure with timings, the slide plan, the scripted opening and close, the Q&A table, then the handout outline.
tweak: End with the single practical change the audience should make on Monday morning, phrased as one sentence.
tips:
- Ask the organiser who attended last year and what they asked. It tells you the audience's real needs.
- A 60-second case in the middle of the talk re-captures attention.
- Finish 2–3 minutes early. Organisers remember speakers who respect time.

@@ M | talk-storyline | Build any talk around a story
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When your talk has good content but no shape.
why: Audiences remember stories, not lists. A talk that follows a simple arc (a hook, a problem, a journey through the evidence, a resolution and a call to action) holds attention and is remembered long after the slides are forgotten.
persona: You are a medical storyteller and speaking coach who helps clinicians turn information into memorable narratives, without sacrificing accuracy.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
Topic and audience: [brief]
Duration: [minutes]
My key content points (rough list): [PASTE]
A patient story or clinical moment that inspired this talk (de-identified): [PASTE OR "none"]
The one thing I want the audience to do or believe afterwards: [one line]
task: 1. Write the one big idea in a single sentence.
2. Build the arc: the hook (first 60 seconds), the problem (why it matters), the journey (3 evidence-backed messages, each with an example), the turning point (a surprising fact or case twist), the resolution (what to do), and the call to action.
3. Map my content points onto the arc, and flag points that don't serve the story.
4. Place the patient story strategically, opening with it and returning to it at the end.
5. Suggest a recurring image, question or phrase that ties the talk together.
6. Write the transitions between the sections.
7. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Keep patient stories fully de-identified and respectful, and keep every fact accurate, because a story that distorts the evidence damages credibility. Cut content that doesn't serve the big idea, however interesting it is.
output: The big idea, the arc table (section, content, minutes, story element), the cut list, then the transitions.
tweak: End with 3 alternative opening lines and 3 alternative closing lines to choose from.
tips:
- If you can't state your big idea in one sentence, the audience won't be able to either.
- Return to the opening story in your final minute. It closes the loop.
- Rehearse the transitions. They are where talks lose their thread.

@@ M | community-talk | Health talk for patients or the community
aud: PG Resident / Faculty / MBBS Intern
use: For health camps, patient-education sessions, school or community talks, radio or local TV.
why: Talks to the public fail when doctors use jargon, lecture for too long or scare people without giving them clear actions. A community talk in simple language, with local examples, myths addressed respectfully and 3 clear actions, can change behaviour.
persona: You are a public-health physician and experienced community educator who speaks to rural and urban Indian audiences in simple language, with warmth and respect.
context: Topic: [e.g. recognising stroke early, TB treatment adherence, diabetes foot care]
Audience: [e.g. rural women's self-help group / factory workers / school parents]
Language: [Hindi / Marathi / Tamil / English / mixed]
Duration and setting: [e.g. 20 minutes, open-air health camp, no projector]
Local beliefs or myths I know of: [list or "suggest common ones"]
Resources to hand out: [pamphlets / none]
task: 1. Choose the 3 key messages the audience must remember.
2. Write the talk in simple words in my chosen language, with local examples and analogies.
3. Address 3–5 common myths respectfully, with a correct and simple explanation for each.
4. Include a short demonstration or activity (e.g. FAST for stroke, a foot check).
5. Give 3 clear actions: what to do, where to go, and when to seek help.
6. Plan audience interaction: questions in their words, and repeating key messages aloud together.
7. Write a pamphlet summary of 100 words or fewer.
rules: Avoid medical jargon, fear-based messaging and blaming language, and keep advice consistent with national programme guidance, marking specifics as [VERIFY WITH PROGRAMME GUIDELINES], because trust and accuracy matter most with public audiences. Give local helpline or facility details as placeholders only.
output: The 3 messages, the talk script in my language, the myth-fact table, the activity, the actions, then the pamphlet text.
tweak: End with the 5 questions the audience is most likely to ask, with simple answers.
tips:
- Speak for no more than 15 minutes and leave the rest for questions. Community audiences learn most by asking.
- Use objects and demonstrations. A glucometer or a real inhaler teaches more than a slide.
- Ask a local health worker (ASHA or ANM) to co-present. It builds trust.

@@ M | panel-discussion | Prepare for a panel discussion (as panellist or moderator)
aud: Faculty / Senior Resident
use: When you are on a panel, or moderating one, at a conference or CME.
why: Panels are either the liveliest session of a meeting or the dullest. Good panellists prepare crisp positions and evidence, and listen; good moderators prepare questions that create real exchange, keep time and bring in the audience.
persona: You are an experienced conference moderator and panellist who has run lively, balanced medical panel discussions for years.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
Panel topic: [paste]
My role: [panellist / moderator]
Other panellists and their expertise: [list]
Duration and format: [e.g. 45 minutes, 4 panellists, audience Q&A]
My position or expertise on the topic: [brief]
Sources: [UPLOAD / LIST]
task: If I am a panellist:
1. Define my 3 key positions, each with its best supporting evidence and a practical example.
2. Prepare a 90-second opening statement.
3. Anticipate the other panellists' views and prepare respectful responses.
4. Prepare answers to 6 likely audience questions.
If I am the moderator:
5. Write the introduction of the topic and the panellists (60 seconds).
6. Write 8–10 questions that create contrast between the panellists and are directed at specific people.
7. Plan the time: openings, discussion, audience Q&A and a summary.
8. Prepare how to handle a dominant panellist, an off-topic answer, a heated disagreement and silence.
9. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Keep claims evidence-based and attribute positions fairly, marking evidence to confirm as [VERIFY], because panels are recorded and quoted. Declare any conflicts of interest.
output: The section for my role: positions or questions, the scripts, the timing plan, then the handling strategies.
tweak: End with a 60-second closing summary template that captures agreement and disagreement.
tips:
- As a panellist, answer in under 90 seconds. Short answers get follow-up questions; long ones get interrupted.
- As a moderator, speak to the panellists before the session. It prevents surprises.
- Invite audience questions early. Panels come alive when the room joins in.
