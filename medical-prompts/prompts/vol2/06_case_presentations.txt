# 2.6 | Case presentations & grand rounds | A good case presentation tells a clinical story in the order the evidence arrived, reasons aloud, and ends with what everyone in the room should learn.

@@ M | case-presentation-master | Departmental case presentation, start to finish
aud: PG Resident / MBBS Intern
use: When you present a case at a departmental case discussion or clinical meeting.
why: Case presentations are where residents show clinical reasoning. A structured presentation (a crisp history, focused examination, a summary, a justified differential, a rational work-up and a clear plan) shows your thinking and invites useful discussion. A disorganised one invites interruption.
persona: You are a senior physician and clinical teacher who has trained generations of residents in case presentation. You value clinical reasoning over exhaustive lists, and you expect every statement to be justified.
context: Specialty and my level: [e.g. MD Medicine, year 1]
De-identified case notes (history, examination, investigations with results and dates as Day 0, Day 2…, treatment and course): [PASTE NOTES]
Time allowed: [e.g. 15 minutes + 15 minutes discussion]
Format: [bedside / slides / verbal]
The discussion point my unit wants: [diagnostic dilemma / management decision / teaching]
task: 1. Write the opening one-liner (age band, sex, key background, presenting problem and duration).
2. Structure the history: presenting complaint, history of present illness in chronological order with pertinent positives and negatives, and relevant past, drug, family, social and personal history.
3. Present the examination findings: general, vitals and systemic, relevant findings first, with pertinent negatives.
4. Write a 3–4 line summary that frames the problem.
5. Give a provisional diagnosis and a ranked differential, each with supporting and opposing features.
6. Present investigations in a logical order, with results interpreted and trends shown.
7. Present the management and course, with the reasoning behind key decisions.
8. End with the discussion points or questions for the audience, and 3 learning points.
rules: Use only facts from my notes, keep everything de-identified, and write [NOT DOCUMENTED] where a key detail is missing rather than inventing it, because the audience will ask about it. Reason step by step when justifying the differential. This is for learning, not a substitute for the treating team's judgement.
output: The full presentation in sections, suitable to speak or to put on 10–12 slides, then the discussion points and learning points.
tweak: End with the 5 questions the chairperson is most likely to ask, and short, evidence-aware answers marked [VERIFY] where guideline-dependent.
tips:
- Present in the order the information became available. It lets the audience reason along with you.
- Pertinent negatives are as important as positives. They show you considered the differential.
- Rehearse the summary until you can say it without notes. It's the part everyone remembers.

@@ M | grand-rounds-master | Grand rounds presentation
aud: Senior Resident / Faculty
use: When you present at hospital or departmental grand rounds.
why: Grand rounds combine a case with a broader teaching review for a mixed, often senior audience. The best ones use the case as a doorway into current evidence, practical controversies and system lessons, and they keep time.
persona: You are an academic physician who has presented and chaired grand rounds for many years. You design sessions that are clinically grounded, evidence-rich and relevant to the whole hospital.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. MD Medicine, year 2]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The case (de-identified summary): [PASTE]
The broader topic the case illustrates: [e.g. drug-induced liver injury]
Audience (departments, seniority): [brief]
Duration: [e.g. 45 minutes]
Sources for the review part (guidelines, key papers): [UPLOAD / LIST]
Guest discussants, if any: [brief]
task: 1. Plan the structure: case presentation (10 minutes), case discussion or discussant (10 minutes), topic review (15 minutes), take-home points and system lessons (5 minutes), and Q&A.
2. Condense the case into a presentation with a clear clinical question.
3. Build the topic review from my sources: epidemiology, mechanisms relevant to the case, diagnosis, management and current evidence, with slide titles.
4. Identify system or quality lessons for the hospital, if any.
5. Write briefing questions for the discussant.
6. Write the take-home points.
7. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Base the review on my sources and mark guideline or trial specifics [VERIFY], because grand-rounds audiences include experts in the topic. Keep the case de-identified and blame-free.
output: The session plan with timings, the case slides, the review slide outline, the system lessons, the discussant questions, then the take-home points.
tweak: End with the question the most senior person in the room is most likely to ask, and how to handle it.
tips:
- Invite a discussant from another department. It broadens the discussion and the audience.
- Keep the review focused on what the case raised. Grand rounds aren't a textbook chapter.
- Circulate the take-home points afterwards. They extend the reach beyond the room.

@@ M | mm-meeting | Morbidity and mortality (M&M) presentation
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When presenting an adverse outcome, complication or death at an M&M or death-audit meeting.
why: M&M meetings exist to learn and prevent recurrence, not to blame. A structured presentation (the facts, a timeline, the analysis of contributing factors using a systems lens, the evidence, and concrete actions) makes the meeting productive and psychologically safe.
persona: You are a patient-safety physician who runs M&M meetings using a systems approach and a just culture. You are factual, balanced and focused on learning.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The case, de-identified, with a timeline of events: [PASTE]
The outcome (complication or death): [brief]
Local protocols relevant to the case: [list]
Institutional M&M format, if any: [paste]
task: 1. Present the case factually as a timeline, without judgemental language.
2. Identify the key decision points and what information was available at each one.
3. Analyse the contributing factors with a structured framework: patient factors, task and technology, individual, team, environment and organisation.
4. Classify the event (e.g. known complication, possible error, system failure, unavoidable) as a discussion point, not a verdict.
5. Summarise the relevant evidence or guideline standards, marked [VERIFY].
6. Propose 3–5 specific, actionable system improvements with owners and timelines (as suggestions).
7. Write discussion questions for the meeting, including one on how a similar event could be detected earlier.
8. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Use neutral, non-blaming language and no names or identifiers, because psychological safety is what makes M&M work. Use only facts from my timeline, and mark unknowns [NOT KNOWN].
output: The timeline, the decision-point table, the contributing-factor analysis, the classification, the evidence summary, the improvement proposals, then the discussion questions.
tweak: End with one-line "learning messages" suitable for circulating to the department.
tips:
- Ask "what made sense to the team at that time?" rather than "who made the mistake?".
- Track your action items. An M&M without follow-up changes nothing.
- Keep the presentation confidential according to your institution's rules for quality-improvement meetings.

@@ M | interesting-case-meeting | Short "interesting case" presentation with teaching
aud: PG Resident
use: For 10-minute "interesting case" or "case of the week" slots.
why: Short case slots succeed when they are tightly structured: the hook, the key findings, the reasoning to diagnosis, and one or two strong teaching points. Presenters who try to include everything run over and teach nothing.
persona: You are a clinical teacher who coaches residents to present interesting cases in 10 minutes with maximum teaching value.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. MD Medicine, year 2]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The case (de-identified notes): [PASTE]
What makes it interesting: [rare diagnosis / atypical presentation / diagnostic twist / management lesson]
Time: [e.g. 10 minutes]
Audience: [brief]
The key image or result that cracked the case: [describe]
What the treating team would do differently next time: [brief]
task: 1. Write a hook for the first 30 seconds.
2. Select only the history, examination and investigation findings that drive the reasoning.
3. Present the case as an unfolding puzzle: reveal the information in stages and pause for audience guesses.
4. Reveal the diagnosis and exactly how it was confirmed (the test, finding or response to treatment).
5. Deliver 2 teaching points with brief evidence, marked [VERIFY].
6. Add a 30-second "what we would do differently" reflection.
7. Write 3 audience questions to use at the pauses, with the expected answers.
8. Plan 6–8 slides with timings that fit the slot exactly.
7. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Keep it fully de-identified and include only facts from my notes, because interesting cases are often recognisable to colleagues.
output: The hook, the selected findings, the staged reveal plan, the diagnosis reveal, the teaching points, then the slide plan.
tweak: End with one "take-home pearl" for the audience to remember.
tips:
- The pause before revealing the diagnosis is where the audience learns. Don't rush it.
- Show one key image or result, large, at the turning point.
- Finish with what you would do differently next time. It's the most useful sentence in the talk.

@@ M | mdt-presentation | Presenting at a multidisciplinary team meeting or tumour board
aud: PG Resident / Senior Resident
use: When presenting a patient at an MDT, tumour board, or joint medical-surgical meeting.
why: MDTs make major decisions in minutes. A crisp, standardised presentation with the right information for each specialty (radiology, pathology, oncology, surgery) leads to better decisions and saves everyone's time.
persona: You are an MDT coordinator and senior clinician who has run tumour boards and multidisciplinary meetings for years.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
Meeting type: [tumour board / ILD MDT / complex-case MDT]
Patient summary (de-identified): age band, sex, performance status, comorbidities: [PASTE]
Relevant history, imaging, pathology and prior treatment: [PASTE]
The specific question for the MDT: [e.g. surgery versus chemoradiotherapy?]
Patient's preferences, if known: [brief]
task: 1. Write a 60-second standardised presentation: the one-liner, the key history, performance status, and the question.
2. Organise the data by specialty: imaging findings for radiology, pathology findings and markers, and staging if applicable.
3. List the options to be discussed, with the relevant considerations for each.
4. Prepare a template for documenting the MDT outcome.
5. Flag missing information needed for a decision, and who should obtain it before the meeting.
6. Note any patient-specific factors (frailty, social support, finances) the MDT should weigh.
6. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: State staging and markers exactly as in the reports, marking any staging version as [VERIFY EDITION], and include the patient's preferences where known, because MDT decisions must be accurate and patient-centred.
output: The spoken presentation, the specialty data sections, the options table, the missing-information list, then the documentation template.
tweak: End with the 3 questions the MDT is most likely to ask the presenter.
tips:
- State the question for the MDT first, not last. It focuses everyone's attention.
- Have the images and pathology slides ready to display. MDTs stall while people search for them.
- Document the decision and the rationale before leaving the room.
