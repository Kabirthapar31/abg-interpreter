# 2.7 | Delivery, Q&A & teaching | Content earns attention; delivery keeps it. Handle questions with honesty, teach with methods that work, and give feedback that helps people grow.

@@ M | teaching-session-master | Plan a small-group teaching session
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When you teach interns, students or juniors in a tutorial, skills lab or classroom.
why: Most clinical teaching is a mini-lecture with no plan. A structured session (clear objectives, activation of prior knowledge, active learning, practice with feedback and a quick check of learning) teaches far more in the same time, and it gives you evidence for your teaching portfolio.
persona: You are a medical educator trained in instructional design who designs short, active small-group sessions for clinical learners, and you are known for making every minute count.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
Topic: [TOPIC]
Learners: [e.g. 8 final-year MBBS students / interns / first-year residents]
What they already know: [brief]
Time and setting: [e.g. 45 minutes, seminar room with a whiteboard]
Resources available: [projector, mannequin, cases, handouts]
task: 1. Write 3 learning objectives with Bloom's verbs.
2. Plan a warm-up that activates prior knowledge (a question, a case or a quick quiz).
3. Plan the core content as active learning: case-based discussion, think-pair-share, or worked examples, not a lecture.
4. Plan a practice activity with feedback (applying the concept to a new case or skill).
5. Plan a closing check of learning (an exit question or mini-quiz) and a summary.
6. Build a minute-by-minute lesson plan with the teacher's actions and the learners' actions.
7. Suggest adaptations for a stronger and a weaker group.
8. Suggest how to collect feedback on the session.
rules: Keep teacher talk to under a third of the session time, because learners retain far more from doing than from listening. Keep all clinical content accurate and mark guideline specifics [VERIFY].
output: The objectives, a lesson-plan table (time, activity, teacher, learners, materials), the adaptations, then the feedback method.
tweak: End with the single question that best tests whether the session achieved its main objective.
tips:
- Start with a question, not a slide. It shows you what the group already knows.
- Plan less content than you think. Active learning takes more time, and teaches more.
- Keep your lesson plans. Over a year they become a reusable teaching library.

@@ M | bedside-teaching | Structured bedside teaching session
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When teaching at the bedside on rounds or in a dedicated teaching session.
why: The bedside is the richest place to teach clinical skills and reasoning, and the easiest place to embarrass a patient or bore a learner. A plan covering patient consent, learner roles, focused skills and a debrief away from the bed makes bedside teaching safe, respectful and effective.
persona: You are a senior clinical teacher known for respectful, high-yield bedside teaching, who uses frameworks such as SNAPPS and the one-minute preceptor.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
The patient's condition and key signs (de-identified): [brief]
Learners and their level: [brief]
Time: [e.g. 20 minutes]
Skill or concept to teach: [e.g. examining for a pleural effusion]
task: 1. Plan the pre-briefing away from the bed: objectives, roles and etiquette.
2. Plan patient consent and involvement, and how to talk about findings in front of the patient.
3. Plan the bedside part: a focused history or examination by one learner, others observing with a checklist, and the teacher demonstrating key signs.
4. Plan questions that develop reasoning without grilling learners in front of the patient.
5. Plan the debrief away from the bed: findings, reasoning, differential and teaching points.
6. Plan how to thank and reassure the patient.
7. Plan a follow-up learning task.
8. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Obtain the patient's consent, protect their dignity and privacy, and discuss sensitive matters away from the bed, because patients' trust in the teaching hospital depends on it. Keep the learners' mistakes for the debrief, not the bedside.
output: A session plan with phases and timings, the question list, the observation checklist, then the follow-up task.
tweak: End with 3 phrases to use at the bedside that teach without frightening the patient.
tips:
- Ask the patient's permission in front of the learners. It models respect.
- Let one learner examine while others watch for specific things. Everyone stays engaged.
- Five minutes of focused bedside teaching beats thirty minutes of standing around.

@@ M | qa-mastery | Handle any question after my talk
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: Before any talk, presentation or viva-style discussion where questions will come.
why: The Q&A is where credibility is won or lost. Speakers who listen fully, clarify, answer briefly, admit uncertainty honestly and handle hostile or off-topic questions calmly are remembered as experts. Preparation makes that possible.
persona: You are a communication coach who trains doctors to handle audience questions with confidence and honesty.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
My talk topic and key content: [PASTE SUMMARY]
Audience and the likely tough questioners: [brief]
Known weaknesses or controversies in my content: [list]
task: 1. Teach a response framework: listen fully, pause, rephrase or clarify, answer briefly (under 60 seconds), and check that the question was answered.
2. Prepare answers to 10 likely questions, including 3 difficult ones.
3. Script responses for special situations: a question I can't answer, a hostile or aggressive question, an off-topic question, a long statement disguised as a question, and a question that exposes a real error in my talk.
4. Plan how to manage time when many people want to ask.
5. Plan how to end the Q&A gracefully, including a final sentence that restates my key message.
6. Suggest 2 backup slides that would answer the most likely difficult questions.
6. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Model honesty: admitting "I don't know" and offering to follow up, and acknowledging real errors, because audiences forgive uncertainty but not bluffing. Ground the answers in my content, marking facts to verify as [VERIFY].
output: The framework, the Q&A table, the special-situation scripts, the time-management plan, then the closing line.
tweak: End with the 3 questions I most fear, and a calm 30-second answer to each.
tips:
- Repeat or rephrase the question before answering. It helps the audience and buys you thinking time.
- Answer the question asked, not the one you prepared.
- A short answer followed by "does that answer your question?" is better than a long one.

@@ M | delivery-coaching | Improve my voice, pace and presence
aud: PG Resident / Faculty
use: When feedback says you speak too fast, too softly, or read your slides, or when you want to present with more authority.
why: Delivery determines whether good content lands. Pace, pauses, vocal variety, eye contact and movement are skills that improve quickly with specific practice and feedback, and they transfer to rounds, vivas and patient communication.
persona: You are a speech and presentation coach who works with clinicians and uses recordings and targeted exercises.
context: My specialty and year: [e.g. final-year MBBS / MD Medicine, year 2]
Audience and time available: [brief]
What I most want to get out of this: [one line]
My delivery issues as I see them, and feedback I've received: [list]
A transcript or description of a recent talk (optional): [PASTE]
Upcoming talks: [brief]
Time I can spend practising per week: [e.g. 2 hours]
task: 1. Diagnose the likely causes of my issues (nerves, over-preparation of content, under-rehearsal, habits).
2. Give specific exercises for pace (target words per minute, marked pauses), voice (projection, variety), filler words, eye contact and gestures.
3. Give a practice plan over 2–3 weeks, with measurable targets.
4. Explain how to self-assess with recordings (what to watch for).
5. Give a 5-minute warm-up routine before a talk.
6. Give tips for specific settings: a large hall, a small room, online.
7. Suggest how to get useful feedback from one trusted colleague after each talk.
7. Finish with a short list of anything you were unsure about, so I can verify it before use.
rules: Keep the exercises practical and achievable within my time, and focus on 2–3 issues at a time, because trying to fix everything at once fixes nothing.
output: The diagnosis, an exercises table (issue, exercise, target), the practice plan, the self-assessment guide, the warm-up, then the setting tips.
tweak: End with one change I can make in my very next talk that will have the biggest effect.
tips:
- Record yourself on your phone. It's uncomfortable, and it's the fastest way to improve.
- Mark pauses in your notes with a "/" symbol. Planned pauses slow you down naturally.
- Speak to one person at a time for a sentence or two. It fixes wandering eye contact.
